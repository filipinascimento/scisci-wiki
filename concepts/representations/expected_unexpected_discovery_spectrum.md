# Expected-unexpected discovery spectrum

## Summary

Expected-unexpected discovery spectrum represents discoveries by how anticipated they were relative to existing theory, evidence, tools, and community expectations before they occurred.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: discovery, research question, field milestone, technical capability, or scientific claim.
- Typical representation: ordinal continuum from expected discoveries to highly unexpected discoveries.
- Mechanism or measurement target: ex ante predictability of discovery content and timing.
- Empirical signature: some discoveries look like imminent missing pieces, while others reorganize the puzzle or reveal uses in underdeveloped areas.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Provides a conceptual axis for [scientific discovery prediction](../methods/scientific_discovery_prediction.md).
- Separates predictable milestones from unexpected breakthroughs, preventing one forecasting task from standing in for all discovery prediction.
- Links [sleeping beauty](../mechanisms/sleeping_beauty.md), [delayed recognition](../mechanisms/delayed_recognition.md), and [novelty and conventionality](../mechanisms/novelty_conventionality.md) to prediction limits.
- Helps evaluation systems avoid rewarding only work whose outputs are easy to forecast.
- Connects ex ante unexpectedness to [research strategy risk-reward](../mechanisms/research_strategy_risk_reward.md), because strategies that depart from established methods are rarer and riskier.

## Operationalization

- Define the reference point for expectation: prior theory, accumulated evidence, instrument readiness, roadmap status, funding-program aims, or expert forecasts.
- Code discoveries along a coarse expected-to-unexpected scale using contemporaneous documents, reviews, roadmaps, grant calls, or expert elicitation.
- Compare expectation levels with time to recognition, citation trajectories, funding patterns, team structure, and field formation.
- Treat the scale as historical and observer-dependent unless pre-registered forecasts or archival predictions are available.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Clauset, Larremore, and Sinatra (2017) frames discovery prediction around what will be discovered, by whom, when, and where.
- The paper's Figure 1 arranges major discoveries from unexpected examples such as penicillin and cosmic microwave background radiation to expected examples such as gravitational waves, the DNA structure, and the human genome sequence.
- Clauset et al. argue that some discoveries become predictable as theory and evidence accumulate, whereas others appear impossible to predict because they change how the scientific puzzle is organized or reveal new uses in underdeveloped parts of the puzzle.
- The paper uses this contrast to motivate why prediction success in citation trajectories should not be confused with prediction of genuinely unexpected discoveries.
- Verified full-text evidence from Foster, Rzhetsky, and Evans (2015) argues that important findings are often more unexpected relative to available knowledge and established methods.
- Foster et al. connect unexpected findings to rare risky research strategies and show that ordinary citation rewards may not compensate scientists for the publication-risk side of those strategies.

## Caveats

- Post hoc histories can make discoveries look more or less predictable than they were at the time.
- The same discovery can be expected in one community and unexpected in another.
- The spectrum is not a quality ranking: expected discoveries can be important, and unexpected discoveries can remain unrecognized for long periods.
