# Nobel laureate interview corpus

## Summary

The Nobel laureate interview corpus is the qualitative evidence source formed from structured interviews with Nobel laureates about credit, collaboration, recognition, visibility, and career dynamics in science.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: laureate interview, narrated episode, discovery, collaboration, award, or career stage.
- Typical representation: coded interview excerpts linked to scientists, prizes, discoveries, coauthors, institutions, and recognition events.
- Data type: elite-scientist qualitative testimony, often triangulated with publications, awards, diaries, letters, biographies, and citation records.
- Empirical signature: repeated reported mechanisms that explain how recognized scientists perceive and navigate the reward and communication systems of science.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Supplies historical and qualitative evidence for the [Matthew effect](../mechanisms/matthew_effect.md) and [recognition-visibility coupling](../mechanisms/recognition_visibility_coupling.md).
- Helps identify mechanisms that are difficult to infer from bibliometric data alone, including [senior coauthor credit shadow](../mechanisms/senior_coauthor_credit_shadow.md) and [fixed-slot award exclusion](../mechanisms/fixed_slot_award_exclusion.md).
- Provides a discovery-oriented evidence layer before designing quantitative tests such as [multiple-discovery status-visibility tests](../validations/multiple_discovery_status_visibility_test.md).
- Can be linked to prize datasets, coauthorship records, citation indexes, and institutional histories.

## Operationalization

- Preserve interview provenance, date, interviewee role, award field, and whether an excerpt refers to personal experience, observed field norms, or general opinion.
- Code excerpts for mechanism type: credit allocation, collaboration, priority, award scarcity, communication visibility, institutional resources, or career stabilization.
- Triangulate claims against publication dates, author lists, prize histories, citation trajectories, and archival records.
- Avoid treating laureate testimony as representative of all scientists; use it as elite-position evidence.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Merton (1968) states that the paper's conception is based partly on Harriet Zuckerman's interviews with Nobel laureates in the United States.
- Merton combines the interview corpus with diaries, letters, notebooks, scientific papers, and biographies of other scientists, making it an early mixed qualitative-historical evidence layer for science-of-science mechanisms.
- The interviews provide reported cases where laureates describe disproportionate credit, junior coauthor credit loss, multiple-discovery recognition skew, and the visibility tradeoff created by adding a famous name to a paper.

## Caveats

- Laureates are an extreme elite sample and may report norms from unusually visible positions.
- Retrospective testimony can be affected by memory, self-presentation, prize recognition, and field-specific narrative conventions.
- The corpus should not be used as a population estimate without linking it to broader publication, career, and award data.
