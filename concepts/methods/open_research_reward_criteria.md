# Open research reward criteria

## Summary

Open research reward criteria give explicit assessment credit for sharing data, protocols, software, code, materials, and other reusable research tools.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: researcher, project, dataset, software package, protocol, promotion dossier, department, journal, funder, or institution.
- Typical representation: open-practice checklist, repository record, data/code availability evidence, TOP-alignment score, audit-and-feedback report, or responsible-indicator dashboard.
- Method target: make reusable research infrastructure visible in hiring, promotion, tenure, and grant assessment.
- Empirical signature: assessment decisions recognize open data, open code, open materials, protocol sharing, and reuse-enabling documentation.

## Operationalization

- Define which research objects count: data, code, protocols, materials, software, workflows, instruments, or analysis plans.
- Require persistent identifiers, repository links, licenses, documentation, and access conditions when possible.
- Distinguish genuine reuse-enabling openness from minimal or empty availability statements.
- Include open-research records in researcher portfolios and responsible-indicator dashboards.
- Audit whether open-research rewards change practice and whether benefits or burdens differ by field, resource level, or career stage.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Moher et al. (2018) identifies rewarding open research culture as the fourth principle for assessing scientists.
- Table 2 defines open research as including sharing data, protocols, software, code, materials, and other tools.
- The paper states that open research facilitates reproducibility and that behaviors promoting it should be rewarded.
- Moher et al. point to TOP guidelines, registered reports, protocol registration, and TrialTracker-style audit and feedback as implementation supports.
- The implementation discussion notes that promotion and tenure dossiers could include data, methods, materials, software, and code sharing evidence.

## Caveats

- Openness quality varies; a repository link does not imply usable data or reproducible code.
- Some data cannot be fully open because of privacy, consent, security, or Indigenous data-sovereignty constraints.
- Open-research labor can be unevenly distributed across team members and career stages.
- Reward criteria should account for field norms and infrastructure availability.
