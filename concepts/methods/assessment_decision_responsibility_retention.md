# Assessment-decision responsibility retention

## Summary

Assessment-decision responsibility retention is the governance method in which indicators may inform evaluation, but named assessors remain accountable for the final judgment.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: evaluation committee, assessor, indicator dashboard, funding decision, hiring decision, promotion case, or institutional review.
- Typical representation: decision protocol, assessment memo, dashboard-use rule, sign-off record, or accountability map.
- Method target: prevent bibliometric scores from becoming de facto decision makers.
- Empirical signature: metrics are cited as evidence, but the final decision records qualitative reasons, exceptions, and responsible assessors.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Refines [metrics support qualitative assessment](metrics_support_qualitative_assessment.md) by naming the accountability locus.
- Guards against [data-led evaluation inversion](../mechanisms/data_led_evaluation_inversion.md), where available indicators define what counts as quality.
- Gives [responsible metrics](../measures/responsible_metrics.md) a procedural test: people, not dashboards, must own the judgment.
- Links evaluation design to contestability through [evaluation-contestation access gap](../validations/evaluation_contestation_access_gap.md).

## Operationalization

- Require the assessment protocol to state which actors are responsible for final decisions.
- Record when metrics were used, what they were used to support, and where qualitative judgment overrode or contextualized them.
- Include an appeal or contestation route for evaluated parties.
- Audit whether committees cite indicators as evidence or as sufficient justification.
- Compare decisions before and after dashboard adoption to detect responsibility drift.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Hicks et al. (2015) argues that quantitative evaluation should support expert judgment rather than replace it.
- The Leiden Manifesto specifically preserves responsibility with assessors even when indicators are used.
- The same paper treats responsible metric use as a social and procedural practice, not only a technical scoring problem.
- This motif is distinct from indicator transparency because a transparent indicator can still be used as an unaccountable decision substitute.

## Caveats

- Retaining responsibility does not guarantee fair decisions; assessors can still misuse evidence or reproduce hierarchy.
- Overly vague qualitative discretion can also hide bias, so the accountable judgment needs an auditable rationale.
- Automated triage systems require special attention because they can shift responsibility before a committee sees a case.
