# Citation-age backfile retention

## Summary

Citation-age backfile retention uses the publication-year distribution of cited journal items to decide how much historical journal backfile should be retained, bound, digitized, or made available.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: journal, cited-year profile, library collection, backfile, archive, or source-selection policy.
- Typical representation: cited-item age distribution joined to journal-level retention or digitization decisions.
- Method target: align collection depth with observed citation use over time.
- Empirical signature: journals with long cited-age tails require deeper backfiles than journals whose citations concentrate in recent years.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Turns [cited journal citation profile](../representations/cited_journal_citation_profile.md) into a collection-management and archive-design decision.

- Provides a historical counterpart to [online backfile depth](../measures/online_backfile_depth.md).

## Operationalization

- For each journal, count citations by cited-item publication year or age.
- Estimate the depth needed to cover a chosen share of expected use.
- Set retention, binding, offsite storage, digitization, or access priorities journal by journal rather than by broad category alone.
- Recompute as citation patterns, field age profiles, and access technologies change.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Garfield (1972) states that the chronological distribution of cited items can guide the optimum size of journal back files.
- Garfield argues that detailed citation histories allow binding and retention schedules to be rationally established journal by journal rather than for groups of journals.
- The same paper reports that typical cited articles are most heavily cited in the two years after publication, while a meaningful older tail remains visible in cited-year distributions.

## Caveats

- Citation use is not identical to reading, teaching, historical, or preservation value.
- Recent citation-heavy fields can still require deep backfiles for methods, standards, legal, or historical research.
- Retention policies based only on citation counts can underprotect low-citation but culturally or regionally important material.
