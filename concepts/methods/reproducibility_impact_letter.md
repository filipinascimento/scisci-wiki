# Reproducibility impact letter

## Summary

Reproducibility impact letter is a portfolio artifact in which original authors document the value of another scientist's error-detection, correction, or reproducibility work.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: correction effort, reproducibility audit, original article, original author team, or assessment portfolio.
- Typical representation: letter or statement from original authors attesting to the contribution and value of reproducibility work.
- Method target: make correction and reproducibility labor visible in researcher assessment.
- Empirical signature: assessment dossiers include documented error-detection or reproducibility contributions, not only original publications.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Adds an artifact-level method to [portfolio-based researcher assessment](portfolio_based_researcher_assessment.md).
- Provides credit infrastructure for [replication and reproducibility](../validations/replication_reproducibility.md).
- Connects correction work to [retractions self-correction](../validations/retractions_self_correction.md) and [cause-specific correction priority](../validations/cause_specific_correction_priority.md).
- Extends [responsible indicators for assessing scientists](../measures/responsible_indicators_for_assessing_scientists.md) beyond publication counts.

## Operationalization

- Identify reproducibility work that detects errors, verifies claims, or helps correct the scientific record.
- Ask the original authors, when appropriate, to document the value and consequences of the correction effort.
- Include the letter in a structured assessment portfolio with links to the original report, correction, data, and code.
- Separate constructive reproducibility work from adversarial or unsupported claims.
- Track whether committees credit the artifact in hiring, promotion, tenure, or grant decisions.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Moher et al. (2018) argues that scientists who detect analytical errors and help correct published science need recognition.
- The paper says original authors could document the value of such reproducibility efforts, perhaps as an impact letter, for inclusion in an evaluation portfolio.
- Moher et al. frame this as part of building evidence-based approaches to assessing scientists.

## Caveats

- Original authors may be unwilling or unable to provide an unbiased letter.
- Some reproducibility work is valuable even when original authors disagree.
- Committees need criteria for weighing correction labor without discouraging criticism or inflating routine correspondence.
