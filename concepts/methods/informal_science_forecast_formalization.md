# Informal science forecast formalization

## Summary

Informal science forecast formalization turns ordinary judgments by scientists, funders, publishers, and hiring committees into explicit prediction tasks that can be measured, validated, and audited.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: forecast, proposal, manuscript, scientist, hiring candidate, field, or decision workflow.
- Typical representation: institutional judgment reframed as a prediction target with input features, horizon, outcome, and validation metric.
- Method target: make implicit forecasts about scientific success scientifically testable.
- Empirical signature: decisions that already depend on expected future value are evaluated as forecasting problems rather than only expert judgments.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Provides a task-framing layer for [scientific discovery prediction](scientific_discovery_prediction.md) and [SciSci prediction domain maps](scisci_prediction_domain_map.md).
- Connects [automated predictive-evaluation safeguards](../validations/automated_predictive_evaluation_safeguards.md) to pre-existing human forecasting practices.
- Links prediction work to [peer review and gatekeeping](../mechanisms/peer_review_gatekeeping.md), hiring, funding, and publication decisions.
- Clarifies when [predictive bibliographic platform traces](../datasets/predictive_bibliographic_platform_traces.md) are being used to replace, augment, or audit expert forecasts.

## Operationalization

- Identify the informal forecast already embedded in a decision, such as expected manuscript impact, proposal success, or candidate career trajectory.
- Define the forecast horizon and outcome before observing the result.
- Compare human judgment, simple baselines, and model predictions on the same validation set.
- Audit calibration, ranking quality, subgroup error, and downstream policy effects.
- Keep prediction distinct from causal claims about what intervention should be made.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Clauset, Larremore, and Sinatra (2017) states that many predictions by individuals, publishers, funders, and hiring committees are not made in a scientific way.
- The essay asks how the scientific community can know what is predictable and what is not.
- Clauset et al. describe a historical call for a data-driven study of the social processes of science and note that reliance on expert judgment limited earlier progress.
- This motivates turning implicit institutional forecasts into explicit science-of-science prediction tasks.

## Caveats

- Formalizing a forecast can change incentives if the results enter evaluation workflows.
- Some expert judgments include qualitative values that are not reducible to a single predicted outcome.
- Prediction evaluation should not be treated as proof that automated systems should replace deliberation.
