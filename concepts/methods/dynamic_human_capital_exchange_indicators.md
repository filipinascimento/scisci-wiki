# Dynamic human-capital exchange indicators

## Summary

Dynamic human-capital exchange indicators are mobility measures that track ongoing scientist circulation, multi-affiliation, return paths, and exchange intensity rather than static brain-drain or brain-gain counts.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: researcher, affiliation spell, country, region, mobility subtype, return path, or exchange corridor.
- Typical representation: circulation rate, traveler share, return share, directional-mobility matrix, dynamic exchange index, or multi-affiliation indicator.
- Method target: capture human-capital exchange as a network process rather than a one-time migration event.
- Empirical signature: travelers and multi-affiliated researchers dominate the mobile population, making static drain/gain labels incomplete.

## Operationalization

- Reconstruct author-country affiliation histories across publication years.
- Classify researchers into non-mobile, migrant, directional traveler, non-directional traveler, returner, and multi-affiliation cases when data allow.
- Measure flows, return paths, corridor persistence, and affiliation-chain length over time.
- Report separate indicators for stock, flow, return, traveler, and impact-weighted exchange.
- Preserve observation-window and publication-threshold restrictions.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Sugimoto et al. (2017) argues that mobility should be understood as global circulation of researchers, not only brain drain or gain.
- The paper reports that travelers make up most internationally mobile researchers in its corpus, while conventional migrant researchers are a minority of the mobile group.
- Sugimoto et al. call for internationally comparable indicators that capture nuanced human-capital exchange, especially because simple migration counts miss persistent affiliation chains.

## Caveats

- Publication-affiliation histories can miss unrecorded employment, education, visits, and domestic movement.
- Dynamic indicators are sensitive to author disambiguation, affiliation parsing, and database coverage.
- More detailed typologies may reduce comparability across sources if each database exposes different affiliation metadata.
