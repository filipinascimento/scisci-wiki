# Societal-needs assessment alignment

## Summary

Societal-needs assessment alignment evaluates scientists and faculty partly by whether their work addresses public, patient, local-community, or broader societal needs rather than only internal academic status signals.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: researcher, promotion dossier, hiring case, department, institution, funder, patient group, or local community.
- Typical representation: assessment rubric, narrative CV field, stakeholder-impact evidence, patient/public involvement record, or community contribution dossier.
- Method target: broaden researcher assessment beyond publications, grants, citations, and journal prestige toward demonstrable social value.
- Empirical signature: committees explicitly consider whether research questions, outputs, practices, or partnerships address stated societal needs.

## Operationalization

- Define the relevant societal need for the decision context, such as disease burden, public welfare, local community benefit, policy need, or stakeholder use.
- Ask candidates or units to document how research questions, outputs, datasets, tools, or engagement activities address that need.
- Include patient, public, community, or stakeholder evidence where appropriate.
- Distinguish immediate applied impact from longer-horizon blue-sky work whose societal value may be harder to observe.
- Pair the evidence with [portfolio-based researcher assessment](portfolio_based_researcher_assessment.md) so social contribution is interpreted qualitatively rather than reduced to one proxy.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Moher et al. (2018) identifies contributing to societal needs as the first principle for assessing scientists.
- The paper argues that an inward focus on career advancement can produce bandwagon behavior without positive societal impact.
- Table 2 states that academic institutions are part of broader society and that faculty should be evaluated on local and broader contributions.
- Moher et al. give examples such as involving patients in clinical-trial outcome selection and sharing data, code, and materials.
- The authors note that bibliometric indicators usually do not measure these contributions, so assessment criteria need to broaden if such behavior is to be rewarded.

## Caveats

- Societal need is plural and contested; local community, patient, funder, and disciplinary priorities may not agree.
- Blue-sky research can have delayed or indirect social value that near-term impact criteria may miss.
- Evidence of social contribution can be easier to assemble in applied fields with explicit stakeholder infrastructure.
- Social-impact criteria can become symbolic unless implementation is audited and compared with actual assessment decisions.
