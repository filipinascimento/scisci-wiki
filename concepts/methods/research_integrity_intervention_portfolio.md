# Research integrity intervention portfolio

## Summary

Research integrity intervention portfolio is a bundle of policy, training, monitoring, reporting, and incentive reforms intended to reduce misconduct, error, and delayed correction in the scientific literature.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: institution, journal, funder, agency, laboratory, checklist, training program, database, guideline, or reward system.
- Typical representation: intervention bundle, adoption checklist, implementation timeline, audit-and-feedback plan, or before/after correction metric.
- Method target: design and evaluate reforms that improve reliability, correction speed, and misconduct detection.
- Empirical signature: interventions target authors, reviewers, journals, institutions, funders, and oversight agencies rather than relying on one transparency tool.

## Operationalization

- Specify the portfolio components: author and reviewer checklists, statistics and logic training, ethics training, centralized misconduct data, uniform retraction guidelines, independent oversight agencies, and reward-system changes.
- Link each component to an expected mechanism, such as error prevention, fraud detection, notice transparency, shorter correction lag, or reduced perverse incentives.
- Measure both adoption and outcomes: compliance, detection pathways, retraction-cause coding quality, time-to-retraction, post-retraction citation behavior, and unretracted suspected cases.
- Compare adopting and non-adopting journals, institutions, fields, or agencies where feasible.
- Prioritize intervention components with [cause-specific correction priority](../validations/cause_specific_correction_priority.md) when some causes produce greater downstream scientific contamination than others.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Fang, Steen, and Casadevall (2012) proposes checklists for authors and reviewers, improved training in logic, probability, and statistics, stronger ethics training, and centralized misconduct data.
- The same full text calls for uniform guidelines for retractions and retraction notices, novel reward systems for science, and dedicated national agencies such as the US Office of Research Integrity.
- Fang et al. argue that ethics training alone is unlikely to curb poor research practices because retractions and ethical breaches may reflect the incentive system of science.
- This makes the intervention portfolio a system-level design problem rather than a single compliance checklist.
- Fang et al.'s comparison of highly cited retractions further motivates [cause-specific correction priority](../validations/cause_specific_correction_priority.md), because fraud and error may warrant stronger prevention and correction effort than lower-impact cause categories in that sample.

## Caveats

- Intervention adoption does not prove behavior change; outcome audits are needed.
- Increased detection can raise retraction counts in the short run even if underlying misconduct falls.
- Some reforms may shift burdens to junior researchers, smaller institutions, or countries without strong oversight capacity.
