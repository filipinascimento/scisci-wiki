# Mentoring-teaching-service assessment

## Summary

Mentoring-teaching-service assessment gives explicit credit for academic work that sustains research communities but is weakly captured by publication and citation metrics.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: researcher, faculty dossier, department, promotion case, research group, training program, or service portfolio.
- Typical representation: mentoring record, teaching contribution, service evidence, committee-work record, training-support indicator, or qualitative contribution dossier.
- Method target: make community-sustaining labor visible in hiring, promotion, tenure, and grant assessment.
- Empirical signature: assessment criteria recognize mentoring, teaching, service, citizenship, clinical or translational duties, and support for others' grant or research work.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Extends [portfolio-based researcher assessment](portfolio_based_researcher_assessment.md) to academic labor outside article production.
- Provides an assessment-policy bridge to [mentorship chaperone effects](../mechanisms/mentorship_chaperone_effects.md), [nih training support indicators](../measures/nih_training_support_indicators.md), and career-formation motifs.
- Complements [team-science contribution assessment](team_science_contribution_assessment.md) by valuing support and service roles that can be invisible in bylines.
- Helps diagnose [assessment incentive misalignment](../mechanisms/assessment_incentive_misalignment.md) when promotion criteria reward visible outputs while relying on undercredited mentoring and service.
- Supports [responsible researcher assessment principles](responsible_researcher_assessment_principles.md) where reliable science depends on training, citizenship, and community infrastructure.

## Operationalization

- Define which mentoring, teaching, service, clinical, translational, managerial, or community roles are relevant to the assessment context.
- Collect evidence from mentoring records, trainees' outcomes where appropriate, training grants, teaching artifacts, committee service, peer-review service, protocol or grant-design support, and stakeholder engagement.
- Distinguish contribution quality from raw counts of mentees, courses, committees, or service hours.
- Use field- and career-stage calibration so mentoring and service expectations do not penalize early-career researchers or disproportionately burden underrepresented groups.
- Include mentoring and service in [transparent assessment-criteria publication](transparent_assessment_criteria_publication.md) and audit whether committees use the criteria.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Moher et al. (2018) reviews proposals that move away from bibliometrics by assessing managerial responsibilities, academic obligations, mentoring students, teaching, clinical work, trial organization, entrepreneurship, and community outreach.
- The paper notes a team-science framework for biostatisticians that separates scientific contributions from teaching and service contributions, including mentoring in grant applications.
- Moher et al. also describe assessment indicators that include leadership, culture, teamwork, good behavior, citizenship, and interactions with societal stakeholders.
- These examples treat mentoring and service as part of the scientific enterprise rather than as optional background labor.
- Moher et al. call for studying whether new criteria are taken up in practice and whether they induce intended effects.

## Caveats

- Mentoring and service are difficult to measure without inviting superficial counts or biased testimonials.
- Underrepresented faculty can carry disproportionate service and mentoring loads, so credit must be paired with workload audits.
- Service credit should not become a substitute for resources, protected time, or institutional responsibility.
- Teaching and mentoring quality often requires mixed evidence rather than a single metric.
