# Societal impact case studies

## Summary

Societal impact case studies are qualitative evaluation documents that describe how specific research influenced non-academic society, often used in national assessment systems such as the UK Research Excellence Framework.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: research case, institution, assessment unit, impact pathway, non-academic beneficiary, or evaluation panel.
- Typical representation: narrative case document, evidence appendix, panel score, pathway-of-impact account, or portfolio of submitted cases.
- Method target: capture complex societal influence that is hard to represent with standardized quantitative indicators.
- Empirical signature: qualitative accounts emphasize concrete influence beyond academia, but are costly to prepare and difficult to compare across entities.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Provides the qualitative counterpart to [policy document mentions](../measures/policy_document_mentions.md), [patent-paper links](../datasets/patent_paper_links.md), and other quantitative societal-impact indicators.
- Links to [responsible metrics](../measures/responsible_metrics.md) because qualitative evidence can temper overreliance on sparse indicators.
- Provides context for [public and policy attention](../mechanisms/public_policy_attention.md), where policy mentions observe only one trace of a broader impact pathway.
- Creates a validation target for [policy mention context audit](../validations/policy_mention_context_audit.md): mentions need interpretation in terms of actual influence, not just counts.
- Raises comparability problems addressed by [policy mention coverage rate](../measures/policy_mention_coverage_rate.md) and other scalable indicators.

## Operationalization

- Define eligibility windows, research-output requirements, and accepted evidence of non-academic influence.
- Collect structured narratives that link research outputs to beneficiaries, policy changes, products, practices, health outcomes, cultural effects, or other societal changes.
- Use expert panels with relevant academic and societal expertise to judge the credibility and significance of the claimed pathway.
- Record preparation cost, selection rules, and success-story bias because institutions may submit only their strongest cases.
- Pair case-study review with quantitative traces where possible, while keeping the evidence types analytically distinct.
- Use [target-oriented impact metrics](../measures/target_oriented_impact_metrics.md) to keep quantitative traces tied to specific recipient sectors rather than broad claims about all societal impact.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Bornmann et al. (2016) describes REF evaluation panels reviewing four-page case studies that explain how internationally recognized research influenced wider non-academic society.
- The same paper reports that REF 2014 evaluated 6,975 impact case studies with more than 1,000 assessment panel members from academic and societal interest groups.
- Bornmann et al. summarize the scientometric debate: case studies can present complex information but require high institutional effort and can be biased toward success stories.
- In the discussion, Bornmann et al. describe the case-study approach as the most frequently used societal-impact measurement method at that time, while noting that results are expensive, success-biased, and not easily comparable across universities.

## Caveats

- Case studies can be rich but are expensive to prepare and evaluate.
- They often privilege visible success stories and can overlook modest, diffuse, negative, or delayed impacts.
- Panel judgments are not automatically comparable across fields, institutions, or countries.
- Quantitative policy mentions can complement case studies but cannot replace qualitative evidence about how research was used.
