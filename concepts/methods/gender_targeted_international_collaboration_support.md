# Gender-targeted international collaboration support

## Summary

Gender-targeted international collaboration support is a policy-intervention motif in which programmes deliberately expand cross-border collaboration opportunities for women researchers when domestic collaboration portfolios contribute to output or citation disadvantages.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: researcher, collaboration programme, country pair, grant call, mobility scheme, publication portfolio, or citation outcome.
- Typical representation: targeted travel grants, international team grants, network-building fellowships, mentoring exchanges, or randomized encouragement designs.
- Method target: reduce gendered differences in access to international collaboration and its associated citation and resource benefits.
- Empirical signature: supported researchers increase international coauthorship, collaboration diversity, or downstream impact relative to a credible comparison group.

## Operationalization

- Identify fields, countries, or career stages where women have lower international collaboration rates than men.
- Design targeted collaboration support, such as seed funding, travel support, matchmaking, visiting fellowships, or international team grants.
- Use randomized assignment, phased rollout, eligibility thresholds, matched comparisons, or difference-in-differences when randomization is not feasible.
- Measure international coauthorship, repeated collaboration, team diversity, publication output, citation impact, retention, and career progression.
- Audit whether the programme creates additional workload, excludes caregivers, or benefits only already advantaged researchers.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Lariviere et al. (2013) reports that in the 50 most productive countries, female collaborations are more domestically oriented than male collaborations from the same country.
- The paper argues that women profit less from the extra citations that international collaborations accrue.
- Lariviere et al. suggest that programmes fostering international collaboration for female researchers might help level the playing field.
- The authors also caution that no simple programme can solve the broader imbalance, because local and historical forces reproduce gender inequality in science.

## Caveats

- International collaboration support is an intervention hypothesis, not a validated universal solution.
- Collaboration access can be constrained by caregiving, visa rules, institutional resources, language, field norms, and local safety.
- More international coauthorship is not automatically better if it increases burden, tokenism, or dependence on already powerful partners.
- Policy evaluation must track heterogeneous effects by country, field, career stage, and institutional status.
