# International mobility typologies

## Summary

International mobility typologies classify mobile scientists by how their country affiliations change over time, distinguishing migrants, travelers, returnees, producers, recruiters, cultivators, and related roles.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: scientist, country, country-of-origin proxy, country-of-work, affiliation chain, or mobility spell.
- Typical representation: mobility category, origin-destination table, return-rate table, or country-role typology.
- Mechanism or measurement target: brain drain, brain circulation, talent attraction, return mobility, and scientific origin versus current affiliation.
- Empirical signature: category shares for non-mobile, migrant, traveler, returnee, immigrant, emigrant, producer, recruiter, cultivator, or incubator roles.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Turns broad [scientific mobility](../mechanisms/scientific_mobility.md) into a reusable measurement vocabulary.
- Helps compare survey-based country-of-origin measures with publication-affiliation measures.
- Supports policy analyses of border restrictions, talent attraction, return programs, and international collaboration.
- Splits into lower-level motifs for [migrant-traveler mobility split](migrant_traveler_mobility_split.md), [country mobility role typology](country_mobility_role_typology.md), and [country of scientific origin proxy](../methods/country_scientific_origin_proxy.md).
- Uses [origin-destination mobility matrices](../representations/origin_destination_mobility_matrix.md) as a tabular bridge between individual mobility labels and country-level mobility roles.

## Operationalization

- Define scientific origin from birth country, country at age 18, first publication country, or another documented proxy.
- Track current or subsequent country affiliations.
- Classify scientists into non-mobile, migrants, travelers, returnees, immigrants, emigrants, and country-role categories.
- Report category shares and return rates by country, field, career stage, and citation-impact group.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Franzoni et al. (2012) uses the GlobSci survey to compare immigration, emigration, and return patterns among research scientists in 16 countries.
- The paper shows why typologies matter: the survey captures immigrants working in core countries, scientists from core countries working elsewhere, and scientists with past international experience who returned.
- Franzoni et al. also make the country frame and response process visible through [core-country scientist panel](../datasets/core_country_scientist_panel.md), [corresponding-author survey frame](../methods/corresponding_author_survey_frame.md), and [survey response-rate heterogeneity](../validations/survey_response_rate_heterogeneity.md).
- Franzoni et al. now supports separate survey-based operational motifs for [foreign-born scientist share](foreign_born_scientist_share.md), [scientific emigration rate](scientific_emigration_rate.md), [scientist return mobility rate](scientist_return_mobility_rate.md), and [immigrant source-country concentration](immigrant_source_country_concentration.md).
- Verified full-text evidence from Sugimoto et al. (2017) classifies mobility from publication affiliations, distinguishing migrants who discontinue prior country ties from travelers who retain their country of scientific origin while adding international affiliations.
- Sugimoto et al. also classify countries by roles in high-impact mobility, including producers, cultivators, recruiters, and incubators, and report that mobile scholars have higher average citation rates than non-mobile scholars.
- The Sugimoto evidence now supports split-out pages for migrant/traveler categories, country-role labels, circulation networks, and mobile-scholar citation premium.

## Caveats

- Country of birth, country at age 18, and first-publication country are not interchangeable.
- Publication affiliations can overcount nominal mobility or miss undocumented migration.
- Mobility categories are descriptive; causal claims need matched or quasi-experimental designs.
