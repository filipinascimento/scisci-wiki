# Incipient field takeoff latency

## Summary

Incipient field takeoff latency measures the long field-level interval between early references or latent interest and rapid growth in active authors.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: field, early reference, author-count trajectory, breakthrough period, or takeoff point.
- Typical representation: simmering interval, pre-takeoff duration, growth-regime breakpoint, or delayed-onset parameterization.
- Measurement target: how long a field remains in a low-growth state before recruitment accelerates.
- Empirical signature: a field has early conceptual or technical antecedents but sustained author growth begins much later.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Separates field-level simmering from individual [scientific apprenticeship latency](scientific_apprenticeship_latency.md).
- Complements [scientific field recruitment rate](scientific_field_recruitment_rate.md), [productivity-regime shift](../validations/productivity_regime_shift.md), and [field-growth model identifiability](../validations/field_growth_model_identifiability.md).
- Helps annotate [field emergence](../mechanisms/field_emergence.md) cases where foundational ideas predate visible population growth.
- Supports historical validation of field-population models by linking curve takeoff to breakthroughs, funding, infrastructure, or public events.

## Operationalization

- Identify the earliest credible references, prototypes, or historical precursors for a field.
- Estimate the date when author counts, paper counts, or venue activity begin rapid sustained growth.
- Measure the interval between early antecedents and the takeoff point.
- Compare takeoff latency across fields and relate it to training needs, infrastructure, methods, or external shocks.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Bettencourt et al. (2008) describes quantum computing as having references back to the late 1960s and 1970s before gaining momentum through later theoretical breakthroughs.
- The paper reports a long simmering period followed by rapid growth beginning around the late 1980s and mid-1990s.
- Bettencourt et al. model this delayed takeoff by changing susceptible-population growth assumptions, while noting alternative explanations such as multiple exposed classes or time-varying contact rates.

## Caveats

- Early references can be hard to distinguish from true field antecedents.
- Takeoff dates depend on field-boundary definitions, database coverage, and smoothing choices.
- A long incipient period does not identify whether the bottleneck was technical feasibility, training, funding, legitimacy, or applications.
