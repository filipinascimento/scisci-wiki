# Scale-free degree distributions

## Summary

Scale-free degree distributions describe scholarly networks in which most nodes have few links while a small number of nodes accumulate many links, producing a heavy-tailed degree profile.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: paper, author, journal, institution, topic, or patent node.
- Typical representation: degree distribution from a citation, collaboration, web, or heterogeneous scholarly graph.
- Mechanism or measurement target: skewed connectivity generated by growth, preferential attachment, fitness, age, or institutional visibility.
- Empirical signature: a heavy right tail in the number of citations, collaborators, links, or references attached to nodes.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Provides a compact diagnostic for unequal attention and centralization in scholarly networks.
- Acts as a descriptive signature before estimating mechanisms such as [preferential attachment](../mechanisms/preferential_attachment.md), aging, or latent fitness.
- Provides the measurement target for the [stationary scale-free growth model](../methods/stationary_scale_free_growth_model.md).
- Connects citation inequality, collaboration hubs, and field-level concentration to broader network-science models.
- Should be interpreted with [growth-attachment null models](../validations/growth_attachment_null_models.md), because degree skew alone does not identify its mechanism.

## Operationalization

- Reconstruct a graph with a stable node definition and time window.
- Count degree, in-degree, out-degree, weighted degree, or projected collaboration degree.
- Compare heavy-tailed alternatives rather than fitting a power law by eye.
- Test whether the distribution is stable across fields, years, and database coverage.
- Quantify [hub dominance in scale-free networks](hub_dominance_in_scale_free_networks.md) when the substantive claim is that high-degree nodes dominate connectivity.
- Compare against [fixed-size random-network baseline](../validations/fixed_size_random_network_baseline.md) and Barabasi-Albert ablations before attributing the distribution to one mechanism.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Redner (1998) shows that citation counts are extremely skewed, with a small share of papers receiving very high citation counts.
- Verified full-text evidence from Barabasi and Albert (1999) introduces the scale-free network framing, uses citation networks as one motivating example, and argues that network growth plus preferential attachment can generate the observed heavy tail.
- The model layer is split out as [stationary scale-free growth model](../methods/stationary_scale_free_growth_model.md), while its operational assumptions are split into [network growth](../mechanisms/network_growth.md), [linear preferential-attachment kernel](linear_attachment_kernel.md), and [node degree growth trajectory](node_degree_growth_trajectory.md).
- The same verified text shows why the scale-free signature is not, by itself, a causal explanation: growth-only and attachment-only variants fail, and real scholarly networks can also include aging, fitness, field boundaries, and database censoring.
- These model ablations are split out as [growth-attachment null models](../validations/growth_attachment_null_models.md).
- Verified full-text evidence from Fortunato et al. (2018) keeps scale-free and heavy-tailed network structure as a background regularity for science-of-science models, but pairs it with richer mechanisms of careers, teams, novelty, and institutional stratification.

## Caveats

- A heavy tail is not proof of a pure power law.
- Degree distributions are sensitive to field boundaries, node disambiguation, citation windows, and database coverage.
- Mechanism tests should estimate attachment, aging, and fitness directly when possible.
