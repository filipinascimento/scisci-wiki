# Country collaboration core

## Summary

Country collaboration core measures the set of countries that form a dense central group in an international scientific collaboration network.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: country, country-country edge, time window, field, or global collaboration network.
- Typical representation: core membership count, core-periphery partition, dense subgraph, or central country set.
- Mechanism or measurement target: expansion and restructuring of the central international collaboration system.
- Empirical signature: a central group of cooperating countries grows or changes membership as international collaboration expands.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Turns [international collaboration networks](../representations/international_collaboration_networks.md) into a compact structural indicator.
- Complements [international coauthorship share](international_coauthorship_share.md), which measures volume rather than central position.
- Links national science capacity, regional integration, and global collaboration inequality.

## Operationalization

- Build a country-country collaboration graph from internationally coauthored papers.
- Choose a core-detection rule, such as strong mutual collaboration, factor structure, k-core, dense subgraph, or core-periphery model.
- Track core size, membership, and edge intensity across years.
- Compare global-core membership with field-specific cores and regional hubs.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Wagner and Leydesdorff (2005) reports that the core of cooperating countries expanded from 37 countries in 1990 to 54 countries in 2000.
- The same full text describes a strong core group of collaborating countries growing from six to eight countries and a broader global network becoming more decentralized.
- Wagner and Leydesdorff argue that the 1990s country network became a global system of reference beyond national systems.

## Caveats

- Core definitions can vary by algorithm, edge weighting, threshold, and counting rule.
- Country-level cores hide institution, city, field, and author-level heterogeneity.
- Core expansion can reflect database coverage and publication growth as well as new collaboration structure.
