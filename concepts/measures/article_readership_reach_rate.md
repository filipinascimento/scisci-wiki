# Article readership reach rate

## Summary

Article readership reach rate measures the share of a relevant scientist population that reads, skims, or otherwise notices a given scientific article.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: article, reader population, field, journal corpus, survey wave, or attention channel.
- Typical representation: percentage of sampled scientists reporting that they read or skimmed an article, or the estimated readership count divided by an eligible audience denominator.
- Measurement target: direct attention to a paper before, alongside, or instead of citation.
- Empirical signature: most articles reach only a very small fraction of the relevant field, making status cues and discovery systems consequential.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Provides a direct attention-denominator layer beneath [recognition-visibility coupling](../mechanisms/recognition_visibility_coupling.md).
- Helps separate paper visibility from later [citation impact indicators](citation_impact_indicators.md).
- Complements platform-derived [reference-manager readership counts](reference_manager_readership_counts.md) with survey or interview evidence about actual reading.
- Helps explain why [author name visibility signal](../mechanisms/author_name_visibility_signal.md) and [attention inequality](../mechanisms/attention_inequality.md) can operate before citation data accumulate.

## Operationalization

- Define the eligible reader population by field, specialty, journal set, institution, or professional society.
- Ask whether respondents read, skimmed, saved, discussed, downloaded, or cited the article in a fixed recall window.
- Estimate reach as observed readers divided by the eligible population, with uncertainty from sampling and nonresponse.
- Keep reading, skimming, saving, downloading, citing, and teaching separate unless a validation supports aggregation.
- Report whether the denominator is individual scientists, journal subscribers, active authors, field members, or platform users.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Merton (1968) treats scarce readership as a central condition for the Matthew effect in scientific communication.
- Merton cites readership estimates indicating that any one chemist reads about one half of 1 percent of chemistry-journal articles, while most psychology research reports reach very small sampled-reader shares.
- The same discussion reports that even the highest-current-readership psychology report reached only a small minority of sampled readers.
- Merton links this scarcity to selection cues: under publication flood, scientists use signals such as the author's professional reputation to decide which communications to inspect.

## Caveats

- Survey readership is sensitive to recall windows, sampled populations, journal coverage, and the difference between reading and skimming.
- Download logs and reference-manager saves may measure access or intent rather than actual reading.
- Readership reach is not a quality measure; low reach can reflect topic narrowness, language, access, timing, venue, or metadata failure.
