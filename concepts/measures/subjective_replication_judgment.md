# Subjective replication judgment

## Summary

Subjective replication judgment records a replication team's substantive assessment of whether its results replicated the original effect, especially when a single quantitative statistic is hard to interpret.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: replication attempt, focal effect, replication report, team judgment, complex design, or outcome classification.
- Typical representation: yes/no replication judgment, qualitative replication note, coder assessment, or subjective outcome column.
- Measurement target: expert interpretation of whether the replication evidence supports the original finding.
- Empirical signature: subjective judgments are reported alongside p-values, effect-size comparisons, and confidence-interval criteria rather than replacing them.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Complements [replication success indicator suites](replication_success_indicator_suite.md) with a substantive interpretation channel.
- Helps evaluate complex designs where [replication effect harmonization](../methods/replication_effect_harmonization.md) cannot fully summarize the result.
- Links [direct replication protocols](../methods/direct_replication_protocol.md) to human judgment while keeping the judgment explicit and auditable.
- Supports [responsible metrics](responsible_metrics.md) by showing why one replication indicator should not become an automatic score.

## Operationalization

- Ask replication teams or independent assessors a predefined question after results are known.
- Store the judgment separately from statistical significance, effect-size difference, and meta-analytic criteria.
- Record whether the original finding was positive, null, interactional, multivariate, or otherwise complex.
- Compare subjective judgments with quantitative indicators to identify disagreement cases.
- Use blinded or independent coding if judgment bias is a primary concern.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Open Science Collaboration (2015) asks replication teams to answer whether their results replicated the original effect.
- The paper motivates this criterion because complex designs, such as multivariate interactions, may not have a simple quantitative interpretation.
- In the aggregate results, 39 of 100 replications received a subjective yes judgment.
- The subjective judgments were similar to significance-testing results but not identical; for example, the paper notes yes judgments for two null replications and a failure judgment for one significant replication when the original was null.

## Caveats

- Replication teams may have expectations, investments, or local knowledge that shape judgments.
- Subjective judgments should be reported with quantitative indicators, not used as a hidden override.
- Complex designs can make subjective interpretation valuable but also less standardized across studies.
