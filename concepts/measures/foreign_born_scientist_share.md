# Foreign-born scientist share

## Summary

Foreign-born scientist share measures the proportion of scientists working or studying in a country whose origin country differs from their current country.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: country, scientist, field, institution, workforce segment, or survey sample.
- Typical representation: percentage of active researchers in a country with foreign-origin status.
- Measurement target: dependence on internationally mobile or foreign-origin scientific labor.
- Empirical signature: high shares indicate that a national research system draws heavily on scientists whose origin lies elsewhere.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Converts [scientific mobility](../mechanisms/scientific_mobility.md) into a country-level workforce composition measure.
- Uses [country-at-18 origin proxy](../methods/country_at_18_origin_proxy.md) in survey data or alternative origin labels in administrative data.
- Complements [scientific emigration rate](scientific_emigration_rate.md), which measures scientists from a country working elsewhere.
- Links to source-diversity measures such as [immigrant source-country concentration](immigrant_source_country_concentration.md).

## Operationalization

- Define each scientist's origin country using birth country, country at age 18, first publication country, or another documented proxy.
- Define the current country of work, study, or affiliation.
- Compute the proportion of scientists in a current country whose origin differs from that current country.
- Stratify by field, career stage, institution type, year, and source country.
- Report whether the measure includes students, postdocs, temporary visitors, and dual-affiliation cases.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Franzoni et al. (2012) computes country-level foreign-origin shares using country of residence at age 18 and 2011 country of work or study.
- The paper reports Switzerland at 56.7 percent, Canada at 46.9 percent, Australia at 44.5 percent, the United States at 38.4 percent, and Sweden at 37.6 percent.
- Franzoni et al. also identify countries with very low foreign-origin shares, including India at 0.8 percent, Italy at 3.0 percent, Japan at 5.0 percent, Brazil at 7.1 percent, and Spain at 7.3 percent.
- The results support the conclusion that the United States is not uniquely reliant on foreign-origin scientific talent among the surveyed countries.

## Caveats

- "Foreign-born" in this motif often means foreign-origin by country at age 18, so the operational proxy should be stated.
- The measure does not distinguish permanent immigration, temporary study, visiting appointments, or dual affiliations.
- Country comparisons depend on field coverage, survey response, and whether students are included.
