# Cross-border training-investment spillover

## Summary

Central science hubs can benefit from educational investments made by other countries when foreign-born or foreign-trained researchers contribute there.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: researcher training origin, current country, and scientific output.
- Typical representation: contribution or impact in destination country linked to training-origin investment proxies.
- Mechanism: human-capital investments spill across borders through mobility.
- Empirical signature: destination hubs gain high-impact output from researchers trained or born elsewhere.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Connects [scientific mobility](scientific_mobility.md) to human-capital externalities and policy debates.
- Useful for estimating whether central countries capture returns from other countries' training systems.
- Links mobility-policy leverage to [foreign-born scientist share](../measures/foreign_born_scientist_share.md) and return circulation.

## Operationalization

- Link training origin, birthplace or early affiliation, current country, output, citations, and public training investment proxies.
- Estimate destination gains and origin-country spillovers while distinguishing temporary, return, and permanent mobility.
- Stratify by field and career stage because training investments and mobility routes vary.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Sugimoto et al. (2017) argues that the United States benefits from network centrality and from educational investments of other countries, citing contributions by foreign-born and foreign-educated researchers.
- The claim draws partly on cited prior work, but the mobility framing makes the spillover mechanism explicit.

## Caveats

- Training origin, birthplace, citizenship, and first-publication country are not equivalent.
- Destination gains can coexist with origin-country diaspora benefits or return circulation.
