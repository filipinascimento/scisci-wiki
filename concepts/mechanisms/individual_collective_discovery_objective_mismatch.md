# Individual-collective discovery objective mismatch

## Summary

Individual career incentives can favor steady, publishable local progress while collective science would benefit from riskier search.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: scientist, experiment, research strategy, publication, novel link, career reward, or collective discovery objective.
- Typical representation: objective mismatch between individual payoff and collective discovery efficiency.
- Mechanism, measurement, or validation target: incentive misalignment in decentralized experiment choice.
- Empirical signature: observed experiment choices can be career-rational but collectively inefficient for discovering new links..

## Uses in Science of Science

- Refines experiment-choice incentives by linking it to [scientific problem choice](scientific_problem_choice.md) and [knowledge network search efficiency](../measures/knowledge_network_search_efficiency.md).
- Useful as a reusable distinction when [central nearby problem choice bias](central_nearby_problem_choice_bias.md) is analyzed in science-of-science data.
- Adds cross-links to [research strategy risk reward](research_strategy_risk_reward.md) so the motif is not read as an isolated result.

## Operationalization

- Compare inferred experiment-choice strategy against a global discovery-efficiency objective such as experiments per new edge.
- Separate individual publication and career rewards from collective knowledge-network coverage.
- Use counterfactual strategies to estimate the discovery loss from local incentives.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Rzhetsky et al. (2015) argues that observed biomedical choices likely support careers requiring regular output but are inefficient for science as a whole.
- The motif makes the objective mismatch explicit before proposing policy interventions.

## Caveats

- The collective objective is modeled as novel-link discovery, not robustness, translation, or error correction.
- Individual incentives are inferred from observed traces rather than directly surveyed.
