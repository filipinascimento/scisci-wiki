# Geographic reach-frequency decoupling

## Summary

Geographic reach-frequency decoupling is the pattern that cross-institutional collaboration can become much more common without a comparable increase in average geographic distance between collaborators.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: institution pair, paper, field-year, region pair, or collaboration edge.
- Typical representation: boundary-crossing collaboration share alongside mean or median geodesic distance.
- Mechanism: collaboration expands by increasing the frequency of ties across university boundaries, both near and far, rather than by simply lengthening each tie.
- Empirical signature: between-school collaboration rises sharply while mean or median collaboration distance moves only modestly.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Refines [geographic collaboration distance](../measures/geographic_collaboration_distance.md) by separating the distance of a tie from the probability that a tie forms.
- Helps evaluate claims that communication technology produces a simple death of distance.
- Links [between-school collaboration share](../measures/between_school_collaboration_share.md) to [status-assortative collaboration](status_assortative_collaboration.md), because partner choice can shift toward status even when geography is less constraining.
- Provides a mechanism for why collaboration networks can densify without becoming uniformly long range.

## Operationalization

- Compute the share of papers crossing institutional boundaries and the geographic distance among collaborating institution pairs.
- Track mean, median, and distributional changes in distance over time rather than only a single average.
- Compare near and far collaborations separately to test whether growth is a frequency effect, a distance-extension effect, or both.
- Stratify by field, institution tier, region, team size, and domestic versus international collaboration when possible.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Jones et al. (2008) shows that U.S. multi-university collaboration grew rapidly from 1975 to 2005.
- The paper reports that average distance increased only slightly: science and engineering mean collaborator distance moved from about 750 to 800 miles, and social sciences from about 725 to 800 miles.
- The authors interpret this as evidence that the death of distance in U.S. science was driven mainly by more frequent boundary crossing rather than a major increase in the length of collaborators' reach.
- The same source connects this frequency shift to institutional stratification, with partner choice increasingly organized by who collaborators are rather than where they are.
- Jones et al. further support a [communication-technology jump test](../validations/communication_technology_jump_test.md) and [regional elite remote-partnership maps](../representations/regional_elite_remote_partnership_maps.md) as complementary ways to separate timing, geography, and status mechanisms.

## Caveats

- Average distance can hide changes in short- and long-distance tails.
- Distance does not capture travel time, communication cost, language, field specialization, or organizational similarity.
- U.S.-only university samples may not generalize to international collaboration.
- Address geocoding and multicampus institutions can distort distance estimates.
