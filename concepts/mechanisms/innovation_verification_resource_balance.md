# Innovation-verification resource balance

## Summary

Innovation-verification resource balance is the collective allocation problem of deciding how much scientific effort should go to novel claims versus replication and verification of existing claims.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: field, journal, funder, research portfolio, incentive system, replication consortium, or scientific claim.
- Typical representation: innovation/verification portfolio share, replication funding program, journal incentive design, or exploration-verification trade-off model.
- Mechanism: novelty incentives accelerate new claims but can leave uncertainty unresolved; verification incentives reduce uncertainty but can be undervalued as unoriginal.
- Empirical signature: fields reward first reports more than replication even when replication would clarify which paths are likely to support cumulative progress.

## Operationalization

- Measure the share of grants, journal slots, awards, or evaluation credit allocated to replication, verification, and robustness work.
- Track whether replication results reduce uncertainty, change belief, detect moderators, or trigger theory revision.
- Compare broad-and-shallow replication panels with narrow-and-deep many-lab designs for portfolio-level evidence needs.
- Model incentive designs that reward both discovery of possible paths and verification of likely paths.
- Treat the balance as field- and maturity-dependent rather than as a fixed universal ratio.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Open Science Collaboration (2015) argues that individual incentives prioritize novelty over replication, making reproducibility less understood despite widespread concern.
- The paper frames single-replication attempts across many effects as broad-and-shallow evidence and contrasts this with many replications of single effects that produce narrower but more precise estimates.
- In its implications section, the paper states that innovation points out paths that are possible, replication points out paths that are likely, and progress relies on both.
- It explicitly suggests that funding, publication, and awards can be tuned to encourage a better collective balance between innovation and verification.

## Caveats

- Too much verification too early can slow exploration of immature ideas.
- Too little verification can let inflated or false claims accumulate as accepted knowledge.
- The optimal balance depends on field maturity, stakes, cost, uncertainty, and the availability of shared infrastructure.
