# Predictable-discovery selection pressure

## Summary

Predictable-discovery selection pressure occurs when funding, publication, hiring, or evaluation systems reward work with immediate and forecastable outputs, pushing scientists away from uncertain discoveries.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: evaluation system, scientist, proposal, manuscript, funding program, field, or research portfolio.
- Typical representation: incentive environment, review criteria, predicted-impact score, short-term output target, or portfolio shift.
- Mechanism or measurement target: selection for projects and scientists that fit existing indicators of likely success.
- Empirical signature: increasing preference for near-term, legible, incremental, or immediately applicable work at the expense of unexpected discovery.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Converts [scientific discovery prediction](../methods/scientific_discovery_prediction.md) into a system-level incentive mechanism.
- Links [prediction feedback loops](../validations/prediction_feedback_loops.md) with [research strategy risk-reward](research_strategy_risk_reward.md), because predictable work is often individually safer than risky search.
- Provides a policy explanation for why high-risk, unexpected, interdisciplinary, or field-forming work may be underproduced.
- Supports portfolio audits that ask whether evaluation criteria reward only discoveries that were easy to forecast.

## Operationalization

- Identify evaluation settings where predicted impact, immediate application, publication count, citation potential, or short-term deliverables shape decisions.
- Compare funded, accepted, hired, or promoted work with rejected or unsupported work on novelty, disruption, interdisciplinarity, delay to recognition, and field diversity.
- Track changes after introducing predictive tools, impact requirements, short grant cycles, or output-linked metrics.
- Use [active science-policy experimentation](../validations/active_science_policy_experimentation.md) or quasi-experimental rollouts where possible.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Clauset, Larremore, and Sinatra (2017) warns that emphasizing predictable discoveries over unexpected ones can breed a more risk-averse scientist.
- The paper connects this pressure to research environments that increasingly require immediate and tangible application of work.
- Clauset et al. argue that data-mining systems based on past success can discourage innovation, exclude novel ideas, and suppress new fields.
- The same essay frames this as an adaptive pressure on the scientific ecosystem rather than only an error in individual prediction.

## Caveats

- Predictability is not always harmful; some scientific goals require planned, cumulative, and mission-oriented work.
- Short-term outputs can be legitimate accountability signals when paired with long-horizon and high-risk portfolio support.
- The mechanism should be evaluated at the portfolio level rather than inferred from one rejected or funded project.
