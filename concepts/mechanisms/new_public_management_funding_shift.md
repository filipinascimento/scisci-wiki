# New public management funding shift

## Summary

New public management funding shift is the mechanism in which science-policy reforms move research support away from autonomous institutional block funding and toward competitive, output-accountable project allocation.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: national funding system, university budget, grant program, project, researcher, or policy reform.
- Typical representation: block-funding share, competitive project-funding share, performance-based allocation rule, or output-accountability regime.
- Mechanism: external accountability and competition change the autonomy, risk tolerance, and selection pressure surrounding research projects.
- Empirical signature: declining institutional baseline support and rising competitive grants create different novelty effects across researcher-status groups.

## Operationalization

- Track institutional block funding and competitive project funding within the same national or institutional system.
- Link funding source to publication, patent, project, or survey outcomes.
- Measure output properties such as novelty, productivity, citation impact, training, and infrastructure support.
- Estimate subgroup interactions by career stage, gender, institutional status, or field.
- Separate policy-level shifts from project-level selection and post-award treatment effects.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Wang, Lee, and Walsh (2018) frames their Japan case through reforms commonly described as new public management: more competition, accountability, and performance pressure.
- The paper uses Japan's declining block grants and rising competitive project grants to compare competitive versus block funding under a mixed system.
- Their results imply that the competitive-funding shift should be evaluated heterogeneously, because low-status groups can respond differently from high-status groups.

## Caveats

- New public management is a broad policy label, so empirical studies should specify the exact funding instruments being compared.
- Competitive funding may increase novelty for some groups while increasing conformity or burden for others.
- National funding systems differ in baseline autonomy, grant size, evaluation criteria, and institutional buffering.
