# Career-security innovation buffer

## Summary

Career-security innovation buffer is the mechanism that job-security slack, decoupled productivity pressure, and person-centered support can make risky original research more individually viable.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: scientist, career stage, lab, employer, funding program, tenure regime, or research portfolio.
- Typical representation: job-security slack, publication-pressure intensity, early-career evaluation rules, investigator funding, or protected exploratory time.
- Mechanism: reducing the career cost of temporary failure makes scientists more willing to pursue risky strategies whose average citation payoff may not compensate for publication failure.
- Empirical signature: risky, bridge, jump, or exploratory projects become more common when productivity pressure is lower or career support is less tied to immediate output.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Refines [funding autonomy and exploration](funding_autonomy_exploration.md) by isolating job security and productivity decoupling from grant duration alone.
- Gives a career-stage policy lever for [research strategy risk-reward](research_strategy_risk_reward.md) and [novelty penalty](novelty_penalty.md).
- Connects [broad-search time policy](broad_search_time_policy.md) to the institutional conditions that let scientists act on broad search.
- Helps explain why [risky-strategy compensation test](../validations/risky_strategy_compensation_test.md) can imply rational conservatism even when science benefits from risk-taking.
- Provides a bridge between people-not-projects funding, early-career security, and [intellectual risk-taking assessment](../methods/intellectual_risk_taking_assessment.md).

## Operationalization

- Measure career-security conditions with tenure status, contract length, lab or employer type, evaluation frequency, protected time, or access to person-centered funding.
- Measure risky search using jump projects, new bridges, novelty scores, distant-topic movement, or exploratory portfolio shares.
- Compare risky-strategy rates before and after tenure, during fellowships, across investigator versus project funding, or across institutions with different publication-pressure regimes.
- Test whether security changes reduce failure-censoring effects by tracking unpublished protocols, grants, preprints, negative results, or later project pivots.
- Separate security effects from selection by controlling for prior productivity, field, resources, institutional prestige, and mentor or collaborator networks.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Foster, Rzhetsky, and Evans (2015) frames research choice as a tension between reliable productivity and risky originality.
- Foster et al. argue that risky projects fail more frequently and can leave scientists appearing unproductive for longer periods, while successful risky projects can have large impact.
- Their risk analysis shows that citation rewards alone are unlikely to compensate for the publication-failure risk of new or jump projects under a simple expected-citation model.
- The paper proposes two policy levers for countering stable conservatism: decouple job security from early productivity and fund scientists rather than projects, with Bell Labs and Howard Hughes Medical Institute as examples.

## Caveats

- Security can support exploration, but it can also protect low-effort or low-value work if evaluation is absent.
- People-not-projects funding can amplify inequality if access is limited to already prestigious scientists.
- Career-security effects are hard to separate from selection, resources, mentoring, and field opportunities.
- Risky projects still need validation, safety, and coordination; security alone does not make all risks socially valuable.
