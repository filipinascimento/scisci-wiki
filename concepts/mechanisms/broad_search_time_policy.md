# Broad-search time policy

## Summary

Broad-search time policy is the intervention logic that scientists need protected time, evaluation slack, and long-horizon support to read widely, keep up with an expanding knowledge frontier, and pursue more disruptive combinations.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: scientist, lab, university, funder program, review regime, sabbatical policy, or research portfolio.
- Typical representation: protected reading time, sabbatical access, grant duration, person-centered awards, output-quantity pressure, or time allocated to broad search.
- Mechanism: time and autonomy lower the opportunity cost of searching outside the narrow slice of prior work that directly serves near-term output.
- Empirical signature: broader cited knowledge, older or less concentrated references, lower self-reliance, and higher disruption when scientists have time and incentives to search broadly.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Converts [knowledge-use narrowing](knowledge_use_narrowing.md) from a diagnosis into an institutional design problem.
- Provides a policy lever for [disruptiveness decline over time](disruptiveness_decline.md) without treating citation disruption as the only evaluation target.
- Connects reading breadth, sabbaticals, and long-term individual awards to [funding autonomy and exploration](funding_autonomy_exploration.md).
- Helps distinguish average disruption decline from the stable right tail tracked by [high-disruption tail conservation](high_disruption_tail_conservation.md).
- Offers a concrete intervention family for [responsible metrics](../measures/responsible_metrics.md): reduce output-count pressure when the goal is frontier-broadening work.
- Complements [career-security innovation buffer](career_security_innovation_buffer.md), which lowers the career cost of acting on broad search.

## Operationalization

- Code policies by whether they provide protected time for reading, field scanning, sabbaticals, and exploratory recombination.
- Measure broad search with [cited work diversity](../measures/cited_work_diversity.md), [reference-age search depth](../measures/reference_age_search_depth.md), [reference-popularity search](../measures/reference_popularity_search.md), self-citation share, and predecessor concentration.
- Compare cohorts exposed to sabbatical policies, long-horizon individual awards, reduced teaching/service load, or explicit quality-over-quantity evaluation rules.
- Test whether broad-search support predicts later frontier-expansion outcomes such as CD5, novelty, or semantic distance after field, year, team, and prior-productivity controls.
- Pair intervention designs with [intellectual risk-taking assessment](../methods/intellectual_risk_taking_assessment.md) and [proposal-burden research-time crowding](proposal_burden_research_time_crowding.md) to separate time for exploration from administrative load.
- Model implementation delays with [exploration retooling lag](exploration_retooling_lag.md) when protected time is spent learning a new area before publications appear.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Park, Leahey, and Funk (2023) argues that broad engagement with the accumulated knowledge stock is necessary for knowledge growth to promote future discovery and invention.
- Park et al. state that relying on narrower slices of prior work can benefit individual careers while being less advantageous for scientific progress overall.
- Their discussion proposes that scholars be encouraged to read widely and be given time to keep up with the knowledge frontier.
- The same full text suggests institutional levers: universities can de-emphasize output quantity, reward research quality, and subsidize year-long sabbaticals; federal agencies can support riskier, longer-term individual awards that give scientists time to step outside narrow research agendas.
- The mechanism is empirically motivated by Park et al.'s finding that papers and patents citing more diverse work, less self work, and older work are more disruptive after controls.

## Caveats

- Broad-search time is a policy hypothesis, not an identified treatment effect in Park et al. (2023).
- Reading widely can produce superficial citation breadth unless evaluation also rewards substantive integration.
- Long-horizon support may amplify status inequalities if access is restricted to already advantaged scientists.
- Disruption is only one outcome; broad-search policies should also be evaluated for reliability, inclusiveness, and cumulative value.
