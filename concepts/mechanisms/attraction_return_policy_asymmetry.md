# Attraction-return policy asymmetry

## Summary

Attraction-return policy asymmetry is the mechanism in which career, team, prestige, and network conditions attract scientists abroad, while return decisions are more strongly shaped by family or personal reasons and only sometimes by job opportunities.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: mobile scientist, origin country, destination country, policy lever, return intention, or mobility program.
- Typical representation: paired outward-move and return-move motivation profiles.
- Mechanism: policies that attract scientists to a destination do not automatically reverse into policies that bring emigrants back.
- Empirical signature: career and research-quality items dominate outward mobility, while return motives emphasize personal or family reasons and conditional job opportunities.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Gives [scientific mobility](scientific_mobility.md) a policy-design mechanism beyond origin-destination flow counts.
- Explains why [scientific mobility motivation taxonomy](../methods/scientific_mobility_motivation_taxonomy.md) should separate outward and return mobility motives.
- Interprets [job-conditioned return intention](../measures/job_conditioned_return_intention.md) as one limited policy-sensitive return channel.
- Helps avoid assuming that scholarship, prestige, infrastructure, or network-building policies that attract foreign scientists will necessarily produce return migration.

## Operationalization

- Survey or code reasons for outward moves and reasons for actual or possible return separately.
- Group outward motives into career prospects, team quality, institutional prestige, network extension, infrastructure, funding, compensation, lifestyle, and family categories.
- Group return motives into family or personal reasons, job opportunities, part-time or late-career return, and no-return categories.
- Compare rank order and effect sizes of outward versus return motives by country, field, and career stage.
- Treat policy levers as asymmetric unless return-motive evidence supports a direct policy pathway.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Franzoni, Scellato, and Stephan (2012) reports that immigrant scientists in every surveyed country ranked future career prospects and outstanding faculty, colleagues, or research teams as the top reasons for moving abroad.
- The paper reports institutional prestige and international-network extension as tied for third place, while family reasons and fringe benefits ranked last for outward moves.
- For scientists with international experience who returned, Franzoni et al. report personal or family reasons as the most important factor regardless of country.
- The authors conclude that policy levers are important for attracting scientists abroad but seem to have a minimal role in bringing migrants back, except where return depends partly on job opportunities.
- This evidence supports treating attraction and return as related but non-symmetric mobility mechanisms.

## Caveats

- Self-reported motives are not causal estimates of policy effects.
- Family reasons can interact with job markets, visa status, childcare, partner employment, and local career structures.
- The mechanism may differ for students, postdocs, senior researchers, and fields with different international labor markets.
