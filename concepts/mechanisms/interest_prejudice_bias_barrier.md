# Interest and Prejudice Bias Barrier

## Summary

Interest and prejudice bias barrier is the mechanism by which financial interests, theory commitments, career incentives, and status power distort what findings are produced, reported, and disseminated.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: field, investigator, stakeholder, peer-review process, or finding.
- Typical representation: social source of bias that increases the probability of distorted positive findings.
- Mechanism: interests and prejudices shape design, interpretation, reporting, and gatekeeping, especially in contested or hot fields.
- Empirical signature: opposing results are hard to publish or disseminate when powerful stakeholders have strong commitments.

## Operationalization

- Identify financial conflicts, intellectual commitments, promotion incentives, and field status hierarchies.
- Track whether studies opposing dominant views face delayed publication, lower acceptance, or weaker visibility.
- Compare fields with different conflict and prestige structures.
- Separate explicit conflict reporting from unreported theory or career commitments.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Ioannidis (2005) distinguishes financial conflicts from nonfinancial prejudice and theory commitment.
- The paper notes that studies can be conducted for promotion or tenure incentives.
- Ioannidis argues that prestigious investigators may suppress refuting findings through peer review and that hot-field prejudice can block opposing results.

## Caveats

- Interests and prejudice are difficult to observe directly.
- Conflict disclosure does not measure the magnitude or direction of bias.
- Some stakeholder investment can improve study size or standards, so effects are not always one-directional.
