# Status-authority evaluation bias

## Summary

Status-authority evaluation bias is the mechanism by which evaluators judge equivalent scientific work more favorably when it is attached to an eminent author, institution, lab, or prior reputation signal.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: manuscript, proposal, paper, author, evaluator, institution, or decision event.
- Typical representation: evaluation outcome conditioned on visible status signals while holding content constant or approximately matched.
- Mechanism: prior status becomes a credibility cue, causing evaluators to read, trust, or classify work differently.
- Empirical signature: anonymized or low-status versions of comparable work receive lower scores, visibility, or acceptance than high-status versions.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Splits a status-bias channel out of the broader [Matthew effect](matthew_effect.md).
- Connects [peer review and gatekeeping](peer_review_gatekeeping.md) to the norm of universalism and to status-aware evaluation designs.
- Provides a conceptual precursor for double-blind review experiments, status-randomization audits, and [reviewer-distance rank reshuffling](../validations/reviewer_distance_rank_reshuffling.md).
- Helps distinguish legitimate reputation priors from illegitimate authority bias in evaluation systems.

## Operationalization

- Compare review outcomes before and after author identity, affiliation, or status is hidden or revealed.
- Use manuscript-resubmission, blinded-review, reviewer-assignment, or audit experiments where equivalent content appears under different status signals.
- Measure status with awards, elite affiliation, prior citations, senior coauthors, lab origin, or named authorship.
- Track effects on acceptance, score, perceived merit, citation uptake, and post-review visibility.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Merton (1968) identifies a dysfunctional form of the Matthew effect: status can become an authority signal that violates universalistic evaluation norms.
- Merton gives the Rayleigh manuscript example, where a paper was initially rejected when detached from the eminent author's name and then judged meritorious once authorship was known.
- The same full text links the mechanism to neglected basic papers by comparatively unknown scientists, including Waterston, Mendel, and Fourier.
- Merton distinguishes this bias from the communication function of visibility: high-status authors can make work easier to notice, but authority bias can distort judgment of the work itself.

## Caveats

- Reputation can sometimes be an informative prior about reliability, but it becomes bias when content is not evaluated on comparable standards.
- Historical anecdotes need modern experimental or quasi-experimental tests before estimating effect sizes.
- Blinding can reduce status cues but may not remove topic, writing-style, institution, or citation-context signals.
