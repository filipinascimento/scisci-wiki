# Global-ranking indicator obsession

## Summary

Global-ranking indicator obsession is the organizational pressure to optimize university status in league tables even when the ranking indicators are arbitrary, inaccurate, or weakly aligned with mission.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: university, department, ranking system, administrator, strategic plan, or evaluation policy.
- Typical representation: ranking-target policy, league-table position, institutional dashboard, or performance-management system.
- Mechanism or measurement target: status competition through externally visible metric ranks.
- Empirical signature: institutional goals, hiring priorities, resource allocations, or public narratives emphasize rank movement over mission-specific evidence.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Extends [indicator systemic effects](indicator_systemic_effects.md) from individual metrics to institutional status competition.
- Provides a failure mode for [mission-aligned indicator selection](../methods/mission_aligned_indicator_selection.md), because global rankings impose a common indicator template across heterogeneous institutions.
- Connects rankings to [institutional prestige hierarchy](institutional_prestige_hierarchy.md) and [faculty hiring networks](../representations/faculty_hiring_networks.md) when status measures shape behavior.
- Supplies a target for policy experiments that reduce ranking emphasis or replace it with local mission dashboards.

## Operationalization

- Identify ranking systems referenced in strategic plans, hiring targets, funding formulas, or public communications.
- Compare ranking indicators with institutional mission statements and local research priorities.
- Audit data quality, source coverage, field mix, and arbitrary weighting in rankings used for governance.
- Track whether ranking pressure changes publication venues, collaboration choices, recruitment, or resource allocation.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Hicks et al. (2015) says universities worldwide had become obsessed with positions in global rankings such as the Shanghai Ranking and Times Higher Education lists.
- Hicks et al. argue that these lists can rely on inaccurate data and arbitrary indicators.
- The article places ranking obsession among examples of research-metric abuse that motivated the Leiden Manifesto.
- The mission-alignment principle directly counters the ranking obsession by stating that no single evaluation model applies to all contexts.

## Caveats

- Rankings can provide rough external visibility signals, but their policy use requires validation against local goals.
- Ranking obsession may operate informally through leadership incentives even when formal policy language avoids rankings.
- A rank improvement can result from changes in competitors or formulas, not institutional improvement.
