# Altmetric audience-filtering hypothesis

## Summary

The altmetric audience-filtering hypothesis proposes that non-citation audiences can help identify relevant or influential papers through online attention channels that differ from scholarly citation.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: paper, online actor, audience group, platform event, or attention source.
- Typical representation: source-specific mention counts interpreted as traces of audience selection.
- Mechanism: different audiences filter the literature according to different relevance criteria.
- Empirical signature: papers receiving attention from non-citation channels partially overlap with but do not replicate highly cited papers.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Gives a mechanism for [altmetrics](../datasets/altmetrics.md) as complements rather than citation substitutes.
- Connects [social web non-citing reader traces](social_web_non_citing_reader_traces.md) to evaluation and discovery tasks.
- Supports tests of [altmetric highly cited filtering](../validations/altmetric_highly_cited_filtering.md).
- Links online attention to [public and policy attention](public_policy_attention.md).

## Operationalization

- Preserve the source category and actor context of altmetric events.
- Compare audience-specific attention against citation-based high-impact sets.
- Test whether source-specific attention improves discovery, filtering, or public-interest detection for a stated user role.
- Avoid collapsing all audiences into a single composite score before validation.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Costas et al. (2015) motivates altmetrics as traces from audiences beyond formal citers.
- The paper tests whether altmetric indicators help identify highly cited papers and finds source- and field-specific performance rather than a universal replacement signal.
- Blog and news mentions show stronger links to high-impact journal attention than many other online sources, supporting heterogeneous audience interpretation.

## Caveats

- Attention can reflect controversy, promotion, teaching, policy use, entertainment, or public salience rather than scholarly quality.
- Source coverage is sparse and platform dependent.
- The filtering hypothesis must specify the intended audience and task before a validation claim is meaningful.
