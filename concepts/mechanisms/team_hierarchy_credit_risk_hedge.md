# Team hierarchy credit-risk hedge

## Summary

Team hierarchy credit-risk hedge is the mechanism by which concentrated leadership can increase immediate visibility and protect credit for lead authors, while reducing leadership and productivity opportunities for supporting authors.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: paper team, lead author, support author, L-ratio bin, citation window, or career-year outcome.
- Typical representation: short-term citation advantage, role-specific productivity split, lead/support benefit curve, or hierarchy-risk interpretation.
- Mechanism: tall teams concentrate agenda control and visible credit, making the work easier to recognize quickly and more rewarding for leaders, but less generative for supporting members.
- Empirical signature: low-L-ratio teams receive more short-term citations and lead authors gain productivity, while support authors do better in flatter teams and high-L-ratio teams gain long-term influence.

## Operationalization

- Compare short-term and long-term citation outcomes across L-ratio bins.
- Estimate productivity outcomes separately for lead and support authors.
- Track whether lead authors on tall teams receive more immediate attention or career output.
- Examine whether support authors in tall teams lose publication opportunities relative to similar authors in flatter teams.
- Pair the analysis with author fixed effects and same-size team controls.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Xu, Wu, and Evans (2022) reports that tall teams increase productivity for lead authors but not for supporting authors.
- The same full text reports that low-L-ratio teams receive more short-term citations, whereas high-L-ratio teams receive more long-term influence.
- Xu et al. interpret tall teams as offering benefits to scientists who lead them and as a hedge against the risk that work will not be credited, while imposing costs on support scientists who do not play leadership roles.
- The mechanism complements the paper's fixed-effect evidence that moving toward flatter teams is associated with broader leadership and productivity opportunities.

## Caveats

- The evidence is observational and does not prove that leaders intentionally choose hierarchy as a credit hedge.
- Immediate citations can reflect field, journal, topic, or resource differences as well as hierarchy.
- Some hierarchical projects may need concentrated coordination for legitimate technical reasons.
