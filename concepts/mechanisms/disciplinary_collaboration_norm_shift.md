# Disciplinary collaboration norm shift

## Summary

Disciplinary collaboration norm shift is the long-run change in a field's baseline expectation that research is done alone, in pairs, or in larger teams.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: field, cohort, author, paper, collaboration network, publication year, or team-size distribution.
- Typical representation: time series of coauthorship prevalence, average team size, or share of authors who have ever coauthored.
- Mechanism: changes in social organization, communication infrastructure, problem type, instrumentation, or evaluation norms make collaboration more or less routine.
- Empirical signature: collaboration prevalence changes over decades beyond what is captured by short-run preferential attachment or triadic closure.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Extends [field collaboration-mode contrast](../measures/field_collaboration_mode_contrast.md) from static field differences to long-run field change.

- Connects collaboration networks to [burden of knowledge](burden_of_knowledge.md), [team assembly mechanisms](team_assembly_mechanisms.md), and changing problem complexity.
- Helps interpret [team-authorship share](../measures/team_authorship_share.md) and [mean team-size growth](../measures/mean_team_size_growth.md) as field-specific norm shifts rather than only aggregate trends.

## Operationalization

- Construct a field-specific publication history with dates and coauthorship.
- Measure the share of papers or authors with at least one coauthor across long time spans.
- Separate publication-rate growth from collaboration-rate growth.
- Compare candidate drivers such as communication technology, institutions, instruments, problem type, and authorship conventions.
- Distinguish long-run norm shifts from short-run edge-formation mechanisms such as triadic closure.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Newman (2004) summarizes analyses of the Mathematical Reviews collaboration network over time.
- The paper reports that mathematics publication rates increased only slightly over about 50 years, while the level of collaboration increased much more strongly.
- Newman notes that from 1940 through the late 1950s fewer than half of mathematicians had ever coauthored, while by the paper's contemporary period virtually all had.
- The paper suggests that changes in social organization, communication, problem types, and approaches may have made modern mathematics more amenable to collaborative investigation.

## Caveats

- Long-run source coverage, author-name practices, and field definitions can mimic norm shifts.
- A rising coauthorship rate does not prove equal contribution or close collaboration.
- Field-level norm shifts can mask subfield heterogeneity and cohort effects.
