# Outcome-measure selection flexibility

## Summary

Outcome-measure selection flexibility is the mechanism in which researchers collect multiple dependent variables, transformations, or composites and then report the outcome path that best supports a claim.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: dependent variable, outcome family, scale item, transformed measure, composite score, or reported hypothesis test.
- Typical representation: outcome inventory, multiverse table, specification curve, family of tests, or disclosure checklist.
- Mechanism: multiple plausible outcome choices raise the chance that at least one reported analysis crosses a significance threshold.
- Empirical signature: collected measures are omitted, the focal outcome differs from protocols or earlier studies, or only one favorable composite is emphasized.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Splits one concrete path out of [researcher degrees of freedom](../validations/researcher_degrees_of_freedom.md).
- Connects [selective reporting and publication bias](publication_bias_selective_reporting.md) to paper-level outcome choice.
- Supplies an audit target for [analytic flexibility and false-positive risk](../validations/analytic_flexibility_false_positives.md), preregistration checks, and replication planning.
- Motivates the all-variables portion of the [researcher-degrees disclosure checklist](../methods/researcher_degrees_disclosure_checklist.md).

## Operationalization

- Enumerate all collected outcomes, scale items, transformations, and composite scores before reading the reported results.
- Compare planned primary outcomes with manuscript outcomes and appendices.
- Recompute results for each plausible outcome and composite, recording how often the claim depends on one selected measure.
- Track correlations among candidate outcomes because low correlations increase the independent testing opportunity.
- Report the outcome family, not only the successful dependent variable.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Simmons et al. (2011) simulates a two-outcome case in which researchers test each dependent variable and their average.
- In that simulation, two correlated dependent variables with correlation `r = .50` raise the p < .05 false-positive rate to 9.5%.
- Simmons et al. note that their estimates are conservative because researchers may collect more than two dependent variables and can combine them in many ways.
- Their demonstration experiment shows how a report can hide many collected variables, preventing reviewers from seeing the outcome-selection opportunity.

## Caveats

- Measuring multiple outcomes can be scientifically necessary when constructs are multidimensional.
- The risk is selective confirmatory reporting, not exploratory measurement itself.
- Outcome families can be ambiguous, so audits should distinguish planned primary outcomes, exploratory outcomes, and robustness outcomes.
