# Assessment incentive misalignment

## Summary

Assessment incentive misalignment occurs when hiring, promotion, tenure, funding, and prestige systems reward visible proxies that diverge from reliable, transparent, socially useful, or creative science.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: evaluation system, researcher, department, institution, funder, journal ecosystem, or ranking system.
- Typical representation: incentive map, reward-policy audit, before-after behavior shift, or proxy-versus-value comparison.
- Mechanism: scientists adapt behavior toward what committees, funders, rankings, and journals reward, even when those rewards do not track the scientific value being sought.
- Empirical signature: publication quantity, venue prestige, grant totals, citation counts, or ranking inputs dominate decisions while complete reporting, openness, reproducibility, and societal contribution are weakly rewarded.

## Operationalization

- List the formal and informal rewards in an assessment process: promotion criteria, grant-review criteria, ranking inputs, prizes, annual-review forms, and prestige signals.
- Compare those rewards to stated values such as reproducibility, openness, patient or public benefit, mentoring, team science, and intellectual risk-taking.
- Audit whether researchers can succeed by optimizing proxy indicators while neglecting the stated values.
- Track pre/post changes when institutions de-emphasize weak proxies and add responsible indicators.
- Treat new indicators as incentives and test for gaming, symbolic compliance, and redistribution of workload.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Moher et al. (2018) states that faculty incentives and rewards are often misaligned with societal needs and disconnected from evidence about reproducibility and the suboptimal publication record.
- The paper argues that common assessments emphasize grant funding, publication counts, citation counts, and journal impact factor because these are easy to obtain.
- Moher et al. contrast those easy proxies with practices often ignored in assessment, including complete dissemination, transparent reporting, open research, data and software sharing, reproducibility efforts, patient or public involvement, and peer-review service.
- The full text links promotion-and-tenure rewards to behavior: if committees do not reward societal contribution and good publication practices, maximal fulfillment of those goals is unlikely.
- Moher et al. also warn that any new assessment measure can fall prey to Goodhart's law once it becomes an optimization target.

## Caveats

- Misalignment is partly field-specific; not every field has the same registry, repository, authorship, or societal-impact infrastructure.
- Some proxy indicators contain useful information, so the problem is substitution and over-weighting rather than quantitative evidence itself.
- Changing criteria can shift burdens toward junior researchers or groups with fewer institutional resources unless implementation is audited.
- Causal claims require policy-timing evidence, comparison groups, and checks for broader secular changes in research practice.
