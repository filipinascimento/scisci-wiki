# Elite publication selectivity signal

## Summary

Elite publication selectivity signal is the mechanism by which eminent scientists' self-filtering, high standards, and selective presentation make their publications more likely to be treated as worth attention.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: eminent scientist, paper, publication decision, research program, reader, or attention stream.
- Typical representation: selective publication, central-idea emphasis, self-censorship of marginal work, and reader expectation of significance.
- Mechanism: high-status scientists often avoid publishing work they judge marginal and present central ideas forcefully, so their names become signals for work that deserves close reading.
- Empirical signature: readers expect a higher average signal-to-noise ratio from eminent scientists' outputs and attend to them more carefully.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Refines [author name visibility signal](author_name_visibility_signal.md) by identifying a behavioral source of the signal, not only its downstream attention effect.
- Extends [eminent-scientist focalization](eminent_scientist_focalization.md) from community attention to elite scientists' selective problem framing and publication choices.
- Links [elite problem-taste socialization](elite_problem_taste_socialization.md) to communication: elite training can shape what is considered worth publishing and how central findings are presented.
- Helps interpret [recognition-visibility coupling](recognition_visibility_coupling.md) without reducing all elite attention to bias.

## Operationalization

- Compare publication rates, abandoned manuscripts, selectivity norms, and self-reported publication thresholds across status groups.
- Code introductions, abstracts, and discussion sections for emphasis on central versus peripheral findings.
- Test whether readers, reviewers, or citers treat papers from highly selective authors as higher-priority signals after controlling for topic, venue, and paper-level content.
- Pair citation/readership data with qualitative evidence on how authors decide not to publish marginal work.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Merton (1968) argues that leading scientists tend to emphasize central ideas and findings while playing down peripheral ones.
- Merton describes eminent scientists as often discriminating between work worth publishing and work that could be published but does not meet their standards.
- The paper gives examples of prominent scientists scrapping or reducing marginal publication output and frames this restraint as a reason their published work is expected to matter.
- Merton connects this selectivity to the communication side of the Matthew effect: community members attend to elite outputs because past average quality has been socially validated.

## Caveats

- Selectivity can be confounded with access to better resources, collaborators, topics, and journals.
- Elite self-filtering can improve average publication quality while also intensifying attention inequality.
- The same mechanism can become dysfunctional if readers ignore high-quality work by less-visible scientists.
