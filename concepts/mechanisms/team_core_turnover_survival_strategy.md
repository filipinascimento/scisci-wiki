# Team core-turnover survival strategy

## Summary

Team core-turnover survival strategy describes how research-team persistence can depend on team size: small teams tend to survive through a stable core, while larger teams require mechanisms for membership turnover.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: research team, collaboration group, field, project, article team, or coauthorship cohort.
- Typical representation: core-team stability, membership turnover rate, team persistence curve, extended-team growth path, or team-size-specific survival model.
- Mechanism: small teams preserve coordination and continuity through stable cores, whereas large teams persist by replacing or adding members as tasks and resources change.
- Empirical signature: small-team survival is associated with core stability, while large-team survival is associated with organized turnover rather than fixed membership.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Connects [core-team Poisson mode](core_team_poisson_mode.md) and [extended-team cumulative-advantage mode](extended_team_cumulative_advantage_mode.md) to a persistence mechanism.
- Adds a survival interpretation to [mean team-size growth](../measures/mean_team_size_growth.md) and [article team-size distribution](../measures/article_team_size_distribution.md).
- Links team-formation models to policy questions about sustaining collaborations of different scales.
- Helps distinguish large-team persistence from a simple increase in average team size or [team impact advantage](team_impact_advantage.md).

## Operationalization

- Track research teams or repeated collaboration groups across publication, grant, project, or consortium histories.
- Measure stable-core retention, member entry, member exit, and total team size for each time interval.
- Estimate team persistence or survival as a function of team size, core stability, and turnover rate.
- Compare small-team and large-team regimes rather than assuming one universal retention rule.
- Pair team-level survival measures with [coauthorship networks](../representations/coauthorship_networks.md) and [team-size distribution decomposition](../methods/team_size_distribution_decomposition.md).

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Fortunato et al. (2018) synthesizes team-science evidence by distinguishing small, stable core teams from large, dynamically changing extended teams.
- The review states that team size shapes survival strategy: small teams last longer when they maintain a stable core, while larger teams last longer when they support membership turnover.
- Verified full-text evidence from Milojevic (2014) supplies the underlying formation model: core teams are small and Poisson-like, whereas extended teams begin as core teams and grow through productivity-linked cumulative advantage.
- Milojevic also shows that core and extended teams coexist, so team growth should be interpreted as a mixture of modes rather than a single replacement process.

## Caveats

- Fortunato et al. present this as a synthesis rather than a new primary survival model.
- Observed turnover can reflect student graduation, grant cycles, facility access, field norms, consortium governance, or author-list conventions.
- Coauthorship data may miss informal contributors, staff scientists, infrastructure roles, and project members who do not appear on papers.
- Stable cores can also become closed or exclusionary, so persistence is not automatically better for novelty or equity.
