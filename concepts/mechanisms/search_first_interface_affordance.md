# Search-first interface affordance

## Summary

Search-first interface affordance is the mechanism by which digital library interfaces make keyword search the default or most salient path, shifting discovery away from browsing.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: archive interface, search box, browse menu, user session, journal platform, or discovery workflow.
- Typical representation: search-first layout, default search button, search-log share, browse/search path split, or interface-level exposure assignment.
- Mechanism: when search controls are placed first or made more prominent than browsing controls, researchers more often retrieve targeted known or query-matching records.
- Empirical signature: search-dominant interfaces coincide with more recent, less diverse, or more concentrated citation and reading paths compared with browse-heavy discovery.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Refines [online search-browse substitution](online_search_browse_substitution.md) by making interface layout part of the causal mechanism.
- Connects [electronic access citation narrowing](electronic_access_citation_narrowing.md) to design choices in digital libraries rather than only to online availability.
- Offers an experimental target: randomize search-first, browse-first, and mixed discovery defaults while measuring downstream citation breadth and age.
- Complements [undifferentiated archive search](../representations/undifferentiated_archive_search.md), where the archive is searched as a large corpus rather than navigated by journal issue.

## Operationalization

- Record whether interfaces place search controls before browse controls, default to full-archive search, or privilege search in keyboard focus and page layout.
- Use logs to estimate search-origin versus browse-origin retrievals.
- Compare reference sets, reading paths, and downloads under alternate interface defaults.
- In observational work, document the platform interface used during the relevant citation-production period.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Evans (2008) states that in online interfaces where searching and browsing are both options, such as ProQuest, Ovid, EBSCO, and JSTOR, the searching option is almost always placed first.
- Evans reports that logs demonstrated more frequent use of search, reinforcing the interface emphasis.
- The paper links this interface shift to broader evidence that papers read through browsing were increasingly replaced by papers found through online searches.
- The full-text citation analyses show that as journal issues came online, references became more recent, fewer distinct sources were cited, and citations concentrated on fewer articles and journals.

## Caveats

- Search-first layout is one mechanism among many; ranking, indexing, recommendations, and link-following also shape discovery.
- High search use may reflect researcher goals as well as interface design.
- Some search systems can intentionally diversify results or route users to older and peripheral material.
