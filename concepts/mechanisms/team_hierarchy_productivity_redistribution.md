# Team hierarchy productivity redistribution

## Summary

Team hierarchy productivity redistribution describes how hierarchical teams can increase productivity for lead authors while lowering productivity opportunities for supporting authors, whereas flatter teams spread productive leadership more broadly.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: author-paper role, team, scientist-year, team-size stratum, or L-ratio bin.
- Typical representation: lead-author productivity curve, support-author productivity curve, role-specific publication counts, or L-ratio interaction.
- Mechanism: concentrated leadership channels credit, agenda control, and output opportunities upward, while distributed leadership gives more team members active ownership of work.
- Empirical signature: lead and support authors show opposite productivity gradients as team flatness changes.

## Operationalization

- Classify authors into lead and support roles for each paper.
- Measure author productivity in the same year as the focal paper or in comparable career windows.
- Estimate lead-author and support-author productivity as separate outcomes by L-ratio.
- Control for team size, career age, and career-age dispersion; where possible, include author fixed effects.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Xu, Wu, and Evans (2022) reports that lead authors are more productive in hierarchical teams with lower L-ratio.
- The same full text reports that support authors experience greater productivity on flatter teams.
- The discussion argues that tall teams benefit scientists who lead them but may impose costs on supporting scientists who do not play leadership roles.
- Xu et al. note that author fixed-effect models suggest researchers' opportunities for leadership and productivity expand as they shift from lower to higher L-ratio teams.
- The same discussion proposes broadening lead roles within existing teams as a possible way to increase innovation and support scientists' advancement.

## Caveats

- Productivity redistribution is observational in the paper; team-role assignment is not randomized.
- Author productivity can be both a cause and consequence of team position.
- The mechanism should not be read as a universal argument against hierarchy, because some projects may need specialized support and top-down coordination.
