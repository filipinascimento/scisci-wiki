# Forced-browsing serendipity

## Summary

Forced-browsing serendipity is the mechanism by which poorly indexed or physically arranged archives expose researchers to adjacent, older, or marginally related work while they search for something else.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: researcher, library stack, journal issue, browsing session, reference set, or field.
- Typical representation: incidental exposure path through neighboring articles, issues, shelves, or table-of-contents scans.
- Mechanism: inefficient browsing imposes local detours that can broaden comparison, historical depth, and analogical search.
- Empirical signature: browse-heavy discovery produces older, broader, or less concentrated reference sets than search-first discovery.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Provides the countermechanism to [online search-browse substitution](online_search_browse_substitution.md).
- Helps explain why more efficient discovery can produce [knowledge-use narrowing](knowledge_use_narrowing.md).
- Suggests interface designs that reintroduce controlled serendipity without requiring inefficient physical search.
- Links archive organization to [reference-age search depth](../measures/reference_age_search_depth.md), source breadth, and interdisciplinary exposure.

## Operationalization

- Compare reference sets or reading histories for browse-first versus search-first discovery sessions.
- Measure incidental exposure with shelf-neighbor clicks, table-of-contents scans, recommendation diversity, or forced exploration panels.
- Track whether browse-like exposure increases older references, distinct cited sources, field diversity, or delayed-recognition uptake.
- Test digital interfaces that surface nearby journal issues, historical neighbors, or semantically adjacent but noncentral work.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Evans (2008) argues that forced browsing of print archives may have stretched scholars to anchor findings more deeply in past and present scholarship.
- Evans states that one chief value of print library research was poor indexing, because title/author indexing within core journals drew researchers through unrelated articles.
- The paper links print browsing and perusal to broader comparisons and movement into the past.
- Evans uses the observed narrowing after online availability as indirect evidence that removing forced browsing changed the reference base.

## Caveats

- Serendipitous exposure can waste time or surface irrelevant work if not paired with good filtering.
- Print-era browsing patterns varied by field, library holdings, and journal organization.
- Digital interfaces can create new forms of serendipity, so the mechanism is about exposure structure, not nostalgia for print.
