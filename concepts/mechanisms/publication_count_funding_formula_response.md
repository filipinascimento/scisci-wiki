# Publication-count funding-formula response

## Summary

Publication-count funding-formula response is the behavioral shift that occurs when institutions receive resources for producing countable papers, making paper quantity itself an optimization target.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: funding formula, university, department, researcher, national assessment system, or publication output.
- Typical representation: before-after policy analysis, quantity-weighted funding rule, output-per-dollar conversion, or publication-mix shift.
- Mechanism or measurement target: goal displacement from research quality or mission to the countable publication unit rewarded by policy.
- Empirical signature: publication counts rise after the formula becomes consequential while quality proxies, venue mix, local relevance, or long-run impact shift adversely.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Provides a concrete example of [indicator systemic effects](indicator_systemic_effects.md).
- Helps design [SciSci quasi-experimental policy evaluation](../validations/scisci_quasi_experimental_policy_evaluation.md) around funding-formula changes.
- Links responsible-metrics principles to observable policy responses rather than only dashboard design.
- Supplies a cautionary case for output-based funding, national research assessment, and institutional performance contracts.

## Operationalization

- Identify the publication-count rule, eligible output types, funding conversion rate, and implementation date.
- Build pre/post publication panels by institution, field, output type, journal quality proxy, citations, and local relevance.
- Compare treated and less-treated institutions or fields when possible.
- Check whether publication growth reflects genuine capacity expansion, salami slicing, venue shifts, or classification changes.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Hicks et al. (2015) uses Australia's 1990s formula as an example of indicator systemic effects.
- The article states that Australian university research funding was based largely on paper counts and that universities could calculate the funding value of a refereed-journal paper.
- Hicks et al. report that Australian researchers published more papers after the incentive, but that papers appeared in less-cited journals, suggesting reduced article quality.
- The later shift to the Excellence in Research for Australia initiative is presented as a policy update after recognizing the effects of the simplistic formula.

## Caveats

- Increased publication volume can reflect real expansion, not only gaming.
- Less-cited journals are an imperfect quality proxy and should be interpreted with field and mission context.
- Funding-formula effects may interact with hiring, field composition, journal expansion, and database coverage.
