# Knowledge-island overexploration

## Summary

Knowledge-island overexploration is the mechanism by which conservative search builds coherent local islands around prominent entities but becomes inefficient as effort repeats known links and overconcentrates nearby.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: knowledge network, entity neighborhood, candidate edge, search strategy, mature field, or experiment sequence.
- Typical representation: local island, repeated known connection, central-nearby search pattern, or efficiency-loss curve.
- Mechanism: individual incentives favor safe nearby combinations around prominent entities, while collective discovery eventually needs broader jumps across the network.
- Empirical signature: observed search focuses on prominent neighborhoods and becomes increasingly costly for discovering larger fractions of the target network.

## Operationalization

- Represent published experiments as edges in a growing knowledge network.
- Measure whether new tests concentrate near high-degree or nearby entities.
- Compare observed local search with strategies that explore farther, lower-degree, or disconnected regions.
- Estimate efficiency loss as redundant tests and repeated known connections accumulate.
- Interpret local islands as useful in young networks but potentially inefficient in mature networks.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Rzhetsky et al. (2015) reports that the MEDLINE strategy tends to explore neighborhoods of prominent chemicals.
- The authors state that this strategy works well for generating coherent, thoroughly explored islands of knowledge in a young network but rapidly becomes inefficient.
- They argue that effort is wasted by excessive focus on a few key entities and repetition of known connections, while researchers rarely wander far or bridge disconnected chemicals.
- They connect this to a conflict between individual incentives for productivity and science's collective interest in maximizing discovery.

## Caveats

- Local islands can be valuable for consolidation, validation, and mechanistic depth.
- Long-distance exploration can be riskier and may fail more often.
- Search efficiency should not be optimized without considering cost, safety, and social value.
