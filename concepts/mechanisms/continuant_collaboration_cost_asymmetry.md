# Continuant collaboration-cost asymmetry

## Summary

Continuant collaboration-cost asymmetry is the mechanism by which newcomers bear more collaboration costs to gain visibility, while established continuants select among opportunities that can increase productivity and credibility.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: continuant author, newcomer, transient author, coauthorship tie, collaboration cost, or reputation return.
- Typical representation: asymmetric exchange between hub-like senior collaborators and less established partners.
- Mechanism: newcomers seek information, access, and reflected reputation; continuants choose partners likely to enhance output and standing.
- Empirical signature: high-status continuants mediate entry and can be selective, while less established authors pay more coordination or opportunity cost.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Extends [continuant gatekeeping](continuant_gatekeeping.md) with explicit cost and return asymmetry.
- Connects [coauthor prestige exposure](../measures/coauthor_prestige_exposure.md) to collaboration economics.

## Operationalization

- Classify authors by continuant, newcomer, transient, or terminator status.
- Measure collaboration costs, author order, output, citation returns, and repeated access where possible.
- Compare newcomer-to-continuant ties with newcomer-to-newcomer or continuant-to-continuant ties.
- Track whether visibility gains accrue disproportionately to entrants while productivity gains accrue to hubs.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Wagner and Leydesdorff (2005) argues that newcomers and transients seek access to senior collaborators' information, recognition, and reflected reputation.
- The same passage states that continuants can choose carefully among many collaboration opportunities.
- Wagner and Leydesdorff cite the idea that collaboration costs are borne by newcomers and transients who potentially gain visibility from working with well-known scientists.

## Caveats

- Cost asymmetry is difficult to infer from coauthorship structure alone.
- Senior collaborators can also bear mentoring, infrastructure, or reputational costs.
- Field norms, author order, and lab organization can change who pays and who benefits.
