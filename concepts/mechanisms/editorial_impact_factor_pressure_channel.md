# Editorial impact-factor pressure channel

## Summary

Editorial impact-factor pressure channel is the mechanism by which editors, publishers, or journal managers push recent same-journal citations because the two-year impact factor has become a consequential target.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: journal editor, publisher, editorial board, journal policy, manuscript review process, or journal-year citation pattern.
- Typical representation: editorial requests, acceptance-bias evidence, publisher pressure reports, or recent same-journal citation anomalies.
- Mechanism: journal-level impact-factor incentives create pressure to raise citations to recently published articles in the same journal.
- Empirical signature: editorial practices favor or request references to recent target-journal papers, especially those counted in the impact-factor window.

## Operationalization

- Audit editorial policies, reviewer instructions, and correspondence for requests to cite recent papers from the same journal.
- Compare recent same-journal citation concentration before and after editor changes, publisher initiatives, or public impact-factor targets.
- Use IFBSCP and related measures to screen journals, then collect journal-specific evidence before making behavioral claims.
- Distinguish editor-requested citation additions from topical special issues and legitimate research continuity.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Chorus and Waltman (2016) lists several editor-side routes to high IFBSCP: editor requests to conditionally accepted authors, manipulated editorials, and acceptance decisions biased toward manuscripts citing recent papers in the journal.
- The paper explicitly mentions that editors may feel pressure from publishers to increase a journal's impact factor.
- Chorus and Waltman interpret the post-2004 rise in IFBSCP as circumstantial evidence for increasingly pervasive journal self-citation malpractices, while emphasizing that high IFBSCP alone is not proof.

## Caveats

- Editorial encouragement to cite recent journal work can be legitimate when it corrects missing relevant literature.
- Publisher pressure is often hard to observe directly from bibliometric data.
- The mechanism should be inferred only with supporting evidence beyond a high IFBSCP score.
