# Indicator systemic effects

## Summary

Indicator systemic effects are the behavioral and organizational changes caused when research indicators become incentives, targets, or allocation rules.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: evaluation system, institution, researcher, journal, funding formula, policy intervention, or indicator target.
- Typical representation: before-after policy change, incentive-response model, gaming audit, publication-mix shift, or goal-displacement analysis.
- Mechanism: indicators alter behavior because actors optimize what is measured.
- Empirical signature: measured output rises while unmeasured quality, diversity, local relevance, or long-run value changes in unintended ways.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Explains why [responsible metrics](../measures/responsible_metrics.md) must treat indicators as interventions.
- Gives [assessment incentive misalignment](assessment_incentive_misalignment.md) the broader mechanism: when indicators are tied to careers, scientists adapt to them.
- Generalizes [citation metric gaming](../validations/citation_metric_gaming.md) from intentional manipulation to broader goal displacement.
- Provides a policy mechanism for [prediction feedback loops](../validations/prediction_feedback_loops.md) and [SciSci quasi-experimental policy evaluation](../validations/scisci_quasi_experimental_policy_evaluation.md).
- Helps design audits for funding formulas, rankings, journal metrics, and institutional dashboards.
- Splits into narrower Hicks-derived cases for [impact-factor publication pressure](impact_factor_publication_pressure.md), [publication-count funding-formula response](publication_count_funding_formula_response.md), and [global-ranking indicator obsession](global_ranking_indicator_obsession.md).
- Journal-level impact-factor incentives are split into [editorial impact-factor pressure channel](editorial_impact_factor_pressure_channel.md), [author preemptive journal self-citation](author_preemptive_journal_self_citation.md), and [post-2004 IFBSCP acceleration](../measures/post_2004_ifbscp_acceleration.md).
- Extends to prediction-heavy systems through [predictable-discovery selection pressure](predictable_discovery_selection_pressure.md) and [risk-averse scientist selection](risk_averse_scientist_selection.md).

## Operationalization

- Identify when an indicator is tied to hiring, promotion, funding, bonuses, rankings, or public accountability.
- Track behavior before and after the indicator becomes consequential.
- Measure substitution effects, such as quantity over quality, venue targeting, local relevance decline, gaming, or field shifts.
- Compare single-indicator systems with plural indicator suites and qualitative review.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Hicks et al. (2015) says indicators change the system through the incentives they establish.
- Hicks et al. warn that a single indicator invites gaming and goal displacement.
- The article gives an Australian funding-formula example: publication counts rose after papers became valuable in funding allocations, while evidence suggested movement toward less-cited journals.
- Hicks et al. argue that systemic effects should be anticipated before indicators are institutionalized.
- The specific publication-count incentive case is split into [publication-count funding-formula response](publication_count_funding_formula_response.md).
- Verified full-text evidence from Moher et al. (2018) applies the same mechanism to hiring, promotion, and tenure: easy indicators such as grant funding, publication counts, citation counts, and JIF can displace assessment of transparency, reproducibility, open research, and societal contribution.
- Moher et al. recommend audit and feedback for institutional adoption and warn that new assessment measures can lose validity when they become optimization targets.

## Caveats

- Not every behavioral response is harmful; incentives can increase transparency, productivity, or neglected forms of work.
- Causal attribution requires careful policy timing, comparison groups, and confounder checks.
- Systemic effects can differ across fields and institutions.
