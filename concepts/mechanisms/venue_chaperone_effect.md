# Venue chaperone effect

## Summary

The venue chaperone effect describes how prior junior-author experience in a specific journal or venue can increase the chance of later publishing there as a senior author, especially in high-impact multidisciplinary venues.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: author-venue history, paper, senior author, junior author, journal, field, or publication year.
- Typical representation: new, chaperoned, and established senior-author categories; chaperone-effect ratio; venue-specific transition probability; or five-year citation comparison.
- Mechanism: senior collaborators transmit venue-specific tacit knowledge, expectations, review norms, and submission strategies to junior coauthors.
- Empirical signature: chaperoned senior authors are more common than expected in some venues, and their papers can resemble established-author papers more than new-author papers in impact.

## Operationalization

- For each journal, classify last authors as new, chaperoned, or established based on their prior publication history in that journal.
- New senior authors have no previous publication in the journal; chaperoned senior authors previously published there only as non-last authors; established senior authors previously published there as last authors.
- Compute the chaperone effect by comparing the observed number of new and chaperoned senior authors with null expectations from randomized author-order histories.
- Compare impact outcomes, such as five-year citations, across new, chaperoned, and established senior-author papers.
- Use [random author-order chaperone null](../validations/random_author_order_chaperone_null.md) and [alphabetical author-order chaperone null](../validations/alphabetical_author_order_chaperone_null.md) before comparing journals across fields.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Sekara et al. (2018) formalizes the chaperone effect and applies it to Web of Science publication histories plus full Nature publication history.
- Sekara et al. report that the effect is stronger in medical, biological, and high-impact multidisciplinary journals and weaker in natural sciences such as mathematics.
- In Nature, they find that chaperoned and established senior-author papers have similar average five-year citation impact, while papers by new senior authors receive substantially fewer citations on average.
- The same full text supports split-out pages for the [chaperone-effect journal panel](../datasets/chaperone_effect_journal_panel.md), [venue PI experience categories](../measures/venue_pi_experience_categories.md), [chaperone-effect ratio](../measures/chaperone_effect_ratio.md), random and alphabetical author-order nulls, and the five-year citation-impact comparison by senior-author category.
- A residual pass separates venue specialization, established-PI share trajectories, external field-panel construction, and restricted-raw/public-derived release design from the broader chaperone mechanism.
- The evidence supports a venue-specific learning and access mechanism, while avoiding direct claims about formal mentorship relationships.

## Caveats

- Last-author seniority is field-specific and can fail under alphabetical authorship, equal contribution, or consortium authorship.
- Prior junior-author venue experience can capture selection into strong teams as well as learning.
- Citation differences by senior-author category can reflect topic, institution, author reputation, review selectivity, and publication timing.
- Accepted publication histories do not reveal submission attempts, rejected manuscripts, or authors who exit before publishing again.
