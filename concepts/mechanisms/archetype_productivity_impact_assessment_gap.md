# Archetype productivity-impact assessment gap

## Summary

Archetype productivity-impact assessment gap is the mismatch between role profiles and evaluation systems that reward publication counts and citation indicators, leaving specialized contributors at a measured career disadvantage.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: scientist, contribution archetype, career stage, publication count, highly cited paper share, or assessment system.
- Typical representation: productivity and high-impact-share comparisons by contribution archetype.
- Mechanism: if assessment systems reward output and citation indicators, role profiles with lower measured productivity or impact shares can face reduced advancement even when their contributions are essential.
- Empirical signature: specialized profiles show lower publication counts or citation-impact shares than leader or supporting profiles in career stages where advancement is evaluated.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Adds an assessment mechanism to [task specialization](task_specialization.md) and [contribution archetype career progression](contribution_archetype_career_progression.md).
- Helps explain [supporting-archetype career attrition](supporting_archetype_career_attrition.md) and unequal progression.
- Links role-aware careers to [responsible metrics](../measures/responsible_metrics.md).
- Provides a bridge between contribution-role data and policy debates about individual-level bibliometric evaluation.

## Operationalization

- Assign scientists to contribution archetypes by career stage.
- Compare publication counts and share of highly cited papers by archetype within each stage.
- Use statistical tests and effect sizes rather than relying only on p-values in large samples.
- Interpret productivity and impact differences as assessment-relevant disparities, not as measures of intrinsic scientific value.
- Connect observed gaps to promotion, funding, retention, or evaluation criteria where those data are available.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Robinson-Garcia et al. (2020) reports that Leader scientists tend to have higher productivity after the junior stage, while Specialized scientists are less productive in early- and mid-career stages.
- The paper also finds that Specialized profiles have lower share of highly cited publications than Leaders and Supporting scientists in relevant comparisons.
- The authors test median differences with Wilcoxon rank-sum tests and report effect sizes with confidence intervals.
- In the discussion, Robinson-Garcia et al. argue that lower productivity and lower highly cited shares can disadvantage Specialized scientists in environments that prioritize publication productivity indicators.

## Caveats

- Productivity and citation indicators do not measure contribution value directly.
- Specialized roles may be essential to team output even when individual bibliometric indicators are lower.
- Assessment gaps can be confounded with field, institution, gender, funding, and team structure.
