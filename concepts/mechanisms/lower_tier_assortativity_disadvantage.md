# Lower-Tier Assortativity Disadvantage

## Summary

Lower-tier assortativity disadvantage is the mechanism by which same-status collaboration benefits elite institutions but leaves lower-tier institutions with weaker access to high-impact partners.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: institution tier pair, multi-university paper, field, period, or collaboration network.
- Typical representation: tier-mixing matrix plus tier-specific impact premium.
- Mechanism: status-assortative matching has asymmetric returns because high-tier closure concentrates strong partners while low-tier closure limits exposure to top-tier resources and visibility.
- Empirical signature: low-tier same-tier collaborations are overrepresented but have lower impact advantages than elite same-tier collaborations.

## Operationalization

- Estimate observed-to-expected tier-pair ratios.
- Estimate impact premiums for same-tier and cross-tier collaborations.
- Compare whether assortativity benefits or harms tiers differently.
- Track whether low-tier institutions lose access to top-tier collaboration over time.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Jones et al. (2008) reports that lower-tier schools also interact within their own tier more than expected.
- The same text states that in-group matching is an advantage for tier I schools but a disadvantage for tier IV schools.
- Impact results show that same-tier advantages decline with rank and can disappear or become slightly negative for tier IV partnerships.

## Caveats

- Lower-tier collaboration may serve local or applied goals not captured by citation impact.
- Tier categories can hide strong departments inside lower-ranked institutions.
- Observed assortativity may reflect opportunity sets, geography, specialization, or funding constraints.
