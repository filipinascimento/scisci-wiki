# Private-interest evaluator bias

## Summary

Private-interest evaluator bias is the mechanism in which reviewers adjust evaluations because a proposal benefits, threatens, validates, competes with, or shifts attention around their own research area.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: evaluator-proposal pair, reviewer field, proposal topic, conflict relation, review score, or funding decision.
- Typical representation: score gradient over field proximity, competitive overlap, school-of-thought alignment, or reviewer-author ties.
- Mechanism: reviewers favor or penalize work based on expected effects on their own resources, status, agenda, or intellectual coalition.
- Empirical signature: score patterns track reviewer interests after proposal quality and reviewer severity are controlled.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Gives [peer review and gatekeeping](peer_review_gatekeeping.md) an agency-based mechanism distinct from [expert cue-sampling bias](expert_cue_sampling_bias.md).
- Provides an alternative explanation for [closest-expert review penalty](closest_expert_review_penalty.md) that must be tested rather than assumed away.
- Connects to [status-authority evaluation bias](status_authority_evaluation_bias.md), [topic identity homophily](topic_identity_homophily.md), and [novelty penalty](novelty_penalty.md).

## Operationalization

- Measure reviewer proximity to the proposal's topic, methods, target field, and potential resource competitors.
- Add conflict-of-interest records, coauthor ties, citations, school-of-thought markers, and institutional links where available.
- Compare private-interest predictions with noisy-signal and bounded-rationality predictions.
- Use blinding, random assignment, fixed effects, and reviewer-proposal distance measures to separate private interest from expertise fit.
- Treat absence of evidence in one setting as setting-specific, not as proof that private-interest bias never matters.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Boudreau et al. (2016) identifies agency problems and private interests as one theoretical perspective for proposal evaluations.
- The full text describes mechanisms such as favoring close research, supporting a school of thought, protecting orthodox theories, discounting competing proposals, or varying effort by personal interest.
- Boudreau et al. report that their observed patterns are inconsistent with evaluators being biased by private interests in their randomized, blinded setting.
- The paper therefore uses private-interest evaluator bias as a mechanism to test against, not as the supported explanation for its main findings.

## Caveats

- Private-interest bias can operate subtly through attention, effort, or standards rather than explicit bad faith.
- Blind review reduces direct identity cues but does not hide topic competition or school-of-thought conflict.
- The direction of bias is ambiguous: proximity can produce favoritism, stricter evaluation, or competitive discounting.
