# Proposal-burden research-time crowding

## Summary

Proposal-burden research-time crowding is the mechanism by which competitive funding systems consume researcher time through proposal writing, reviewing, and project administration, reducing time available for research.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: researcher, grant system, proposal, review panel, project, or funding portfolio.
- Typical representation: proposal submissions per award, review load, administrative time, success rate, or time-budget tradeoff.
- Mechanism: lower success rates and more competitive funding shift effort from doing science toward applying for, reviewing, and managing grants.
- Empirical signature: funding systems with high proposal volume or low success rates impose opportunity costs that should be included in funding-model evaluation.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Adds a cost side to [funding allocation design alternatives](../methods/funding_allocation_design_alternatives.md) and [funding incentives and exploration](funding_incentives_and_exploration.md).
- Complements [funding model status contingency](funding_model_status_contingency.md), because burden may fall unevenly across junior, low-status, or under-resourced researchers.
- Connects resource-allocation policy to [peer review and gatekeeping](peer_review_gatekeeping.md) and [responsible metrics](../measures/responsible_metrics.md).
- Provides a motivation for experiments with lotteries, fellowships, block funding, streamlined review, or staged proposal systems.

## Operationalization

- Measure proposal-writing time, reviewing time, administrative time, application counts, success rates, resubmission cycles, and award sizes.
- Estimate total researcher-hours spent per funded project or per dollar allocated.
- Stratify by career stage, gender, institution, field, and prior funding status.
- Compare burden under competitive project funding, block funding, fellowships, and mixed funding portfolios.
- Include opportunity costs in funding-policy simulations and cost-benefit analyses.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Wang, Lee, and Walsh (2018) notes that competitive project funding costs researchers substantial time for writing proposals, evaluating proposals, and administering projects.
- The paper argues that falling success rates can force researchers to write and review more proposals, crowding out research time.
- Wang et al. present this as a limitation of answering which funding model is better purely through novelty, because broader funding-system evaluation requires cost-benefit analysis.
- Their policy discussion links this burden to the need to balance control and targeting through competitive peer review with flexibility and support for varied scientific perspectives.
- Earlier verified full-text evidence from Azoulay, Graff Zivin, and Manso (2009) describes NIH review as demanding extensive preliminary evidence, repeated resubmissions, and multiple applications that take time away from research activities.
- Azoulay et al. use the HHMI-NIH contrast to show that protected funding can reduce conventional R01 application volume, making application burden part of the funding-incentive mechanism rather than only an administrative cost.

## Caveats

- Proposal burden is rarely measured in the same data that measure project outputs, so direct causal estimates can be difficult.
- Administrative burden can also buy accountability, coordination, or public legitimacy; the mechanism is not a blanket rejection of review.
- Burden varies by grant size, field, national system, resubmission rules, and institutional support.
