# Knowledge-stock disruption asymmetry

## Summary

Knowledge-stock disruption asymmetry separates growth in available prior knowledge from actual use of that knowledge, noting that stock growth can relate differently to paper and patent disruptiveness.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: field, technology class, paper, patent, year, or knowledge-stock measure.
- Typical representation: stock of prior papers or patents compared with disruption scores and knowledge-use breadth.
- Mechanism: the available knowledge frontier can expand while the effective knowledge used by researchers narrows.
- Empirical signature: knowledge-stock growth does not map uniformly onto disruptiveness across papers and patents.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Refines [knowledge-use narrowing](knowledge_use_narrowing.md).
- Adds a stock-versus-use layer to [disruptiveness decline over time](disruptiveness_decline.md).
- Connects [burden of knowledge](burden_of_knowledge.md) to citation and patent reference behavior.
- Helps interpret [cited work diversity](../measures/cited_work_diversity.md) and [top-cited predecessor concentration](../measures/top_cited_predecessor_concentration.md).

## Operationalization

- Measure the stock of papers or patents available in a field or technology class before each focal item.
- Model the association between stock size and disruption.
- Separately measure actual knowledge use through reference diversity, reference age, or predecessor concentration.
- Compare whether stock and use move in the same direction.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Park, Leahey, and Funk (2023) examines knowledge-stock growth as a possible explanation for disruption decline.
- The paper reports a positive association between paper stock growth and disruptiveness for papers but a negative association for patents.
- This asymmetry motivates the claim that availability of knowledge differs from actual use, leading the authors to test narrowing of cited work.

## Caveats

- Knowledge stock is a coarse proxy for available ideas.
- Databases expand over time, so apparent stock growth partly reflects indexing.
- Stock-use asymmetry does not by itself identify why scientists use narrower slices of knowledge.
