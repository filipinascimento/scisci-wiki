# Elite-access stratification trend

## Summary

Elite-access stratification trend is the pattern that access to high-status collaboration partners becomes increasingly concentrated among other high-status institutions over time.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: institution tier pair, field, time period, multi-university paper, or collaboration edge.
- Typical representation: time series of observed-to-expected tier-pair ratios, tier-pair share table, or high-tier access curve.
- Mechanism: prestige, search, reputational sorting, resource asymmetry, and network closure can make elite partners increasingly likely to collaborate with each other.
- Empirical signature: high-high tier collaborations rise relative to a null baseline while high-low tier collaborations fall.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Turns [status-assortative collaboration](status_assortative_collaboration.md) into a temporal stratification motif.
- Uses [random tier matching baseline](../validations/random_tier_matching_baseline.md) and [university tier mixing matrices](../representations/university_tier_mixing_matrix.md) to separate marginal participation from pair-specific access.
- Connects partner selection to consequences measured by [between-school impact premium](between_school_impact_premium.md) and [strongest-partner impact model](strongest_partner_impact_model.md).
- Helps explain how more collaboration can coexist with growing inequality in high-impact scientific opportunity.

## Operationalization

- Rank institutions and assign them to stable tiers or period-specific tiers.
- For each period, count observed tier-pair collaborations and expected pair counts under a documented null model.
- Track observed-to-expected ratios for high-high, high-low, and lower-tier cells over time.
- Compare stratification trends with citation outcomes, geography, funding, field composition, and prior collaboration histories.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Jones et al. (2008) reports that tier I-tier I collaborations rise over time relative to random-matching expectations.
- The same source reports that tier I-tier IV collaborations fall relative to expectations, indicating declining access between top-tier and lowest-tier institutions.
- Jones et al. also report that tier I institutions occupy places in more than half of multi-university collaborations in the 2001-2005 period, despite tier I containing only the top 5% of schools.
- The paper links this access pattern to citation advantage, because the highest-impact multi-university teams are most strongly associated with top-tier universities.

## Caveats

- Tier assignment can change over time, and fixed versus rolling tiers can produce different trend interpretations.
- Declining high-low collaboration can reflect field specialization, geography, grant structures, or capacity constraints as well as status exclusion.
- Observed-to-expected baselines should be stress-tested against degree-preserving and geography-preserving nulls.
- More collaboration among lower-tier institutions may still increase total opportunity even if elite access becomes more stratified.
