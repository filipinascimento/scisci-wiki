# Gender and race stratification

## Summary

Gender and race stratification describes systematic differences in access, recognition, funding, authorship, hiring, mobility, and impact across demographic groups in science.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: scientist, team, institution, field, funding application, paper, citation, or career transition.
- Typical representation: demographic outcome gaps across longitudinal career and publication records.
- Mechanism: bias, network exclusion, differential resources, gatekeeping, cumulative advantage, and structural constraints.
- Empirical signature: persistent disparities after accounting for observable career, field, institution, or productivity measures.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Used to study equity, reward systems, peer review, authorship, funding, and representation.
- Connects attention inequality and institutional hierarchy to social categories.
- Requires careful treatment of inferred demographics and privacy.

## Operationalization

- Link publications, grants, careers, institutions, and demographic data or carefully documented inferred proxies.
- Estimate gaps in funding, citations, authorship positions, hiring, promotion, and mobility.
- Validate inferences and report uncertainty, missingness, and ethical constraints.

## Evidence and Validations

- Ginther et al.'s verified Science full text links NIH IMPAC II grant records, Web of Science records, and applicant characteristics. It reports that Asian applicants were 4 percentage points and Black or African-American applicants 13 percentage points less likely than white applicants to receive R01 funding, with Black applicants remaining 10 percentage points less likely after the reported controls.
- The same verified full text decomposes funding stratification into reusable covariate and process motifs: [R01 application resubmission sequences](../methods/r01_application_resubmission_sequences.md), [R01 cohort comparability window](../validations/r01_cohort_comparability_window.md), [NIH training support indicators](../measures/nih_training_support_indicators.md), [training-support disparity persistence](../validations/training_support_disparity_persistence.md), [PhD-citizenship stratification](../measures/phd_citizenship_stratification.md), [institutional NIH funding rank](../measures/institutional_nih_funding_rank.md), [prior NIH experience indicators](../measures/prior_nih_experience_indicators.md), [applicant bibliometric control vectors](../methods/applicant_bibliometric_control_vector.md), [conservative applicant-publication matching bias](../validations/conservative_applicant_publication_matching_bias.md), and [priority score receipt](../measures/priority_score_receipt.md).
- Verified full-text evidence from Lariviere et al. (2013) adds a global publication layer: their [WoS global gender bibliometric panel](../datasets/wos_global_gender_bibliometric_panel.md) covers 5,483,841 articles and reviews and 27,329,915 authorships from 2008-2012, with inferred gender, country, discipline, collaboration, and citation layers.
- Lariviere et al. split this layer into [gendered fractional authorship ratio](../measures/gendered_fractional_authorship_ratio.md), [gendered author-position gap](../measures/gendered_author_position_gap.md), [gendered collaboration rate](../measures/gendered_collaboration_rate.md), and [lead-author gender citation gap](lead_author_gender_citation_gap.md), showing that women are underrepresented in fractionalized authorships, have more domestic collaboration portfolios, and receive lower citations in prominent author positions.
- The same evidence also motivates [gender parity pocket maps](../representations/gender_parity_pocket_maps.md), [gendered discipline output sorting](../measures/gendered_discipline_output_sorting.md), [low-output parity-pocket caveat](../validations/low_output_parity_pocket_caveat.md), [workforce-output parity divergence](../validations/workforce_output_parity_divergence.md), [gendered seniority-pipeline confound](../validations/gendered_seniority_pipeline_confound.md), [authorship-output indicator boundary](../validations/authorship_output_indicator_boundary.md), and [citation-evaluation disparity feedback](citation_evaluation_disparity_feedback.md).
- Lariviere et al. also frame local historical, social, cultural, economic, and policy contexts as candidates for [local gender-disparity micro-mechanisms](local_gender_disparity_micro_mechanisms.md).
- The same verified supplement makes [given-name gender assignment](../methods/given_name_gender_assignment.md) and [gender-name assignment validation](../validations/gender_name_assignment_validation.md) necessary guardrails for interpreting gender-stratified bibliometrics.
- Verified full-text evidence from King et al. (2017) adds a citation-behavior layer: in 1.5 million JSTOR papers, men self-cited more often than women, making self-citation a possible contributor to gendered visibility and cumulative advantage.
- Kozlowski et al.'s verified PNAS full text connects intersectional identities to topics and impact, finding identity-topic homophily and both between-topic and within-topic citation disadvantages for minoritized scientists.
- Kozlowski et al. also provide [race-gender topic portfolio maps](../representations/race_gender_topic_portfolio_maps.md) and a [demographic parity topic-output counterfactual](../validations/demographic_parity_topic_output_counterfactual.md), showing that stratification can alter what topics receive scientific effort.
- These papers make the motif explicitly multilayer: grant evaluation, topic allocation, citation attention, demographic representation, and institutional access interact with [peer review gatekeeping](peer_review_gatekeeping.md) and [attention inequality](attention_inequality.md).
- More specific linked motifs split this broad page into reusable analytic pieces: [NIH R01 award disparities](../validations/nih_r01_award_disparities.md), [intersectional topic stratification](intersectional_topic_stratification.md), and [name-based demographic inference](../methods/name_based_demographic_inference.md).
- Verified full-text evidence from Wapman et al. (2022) adds a faculty-market layer: women's representation among newly hired faculty is often flat or below parity, self-hiring differs by gender in some fields, and [faculty gender demographic turnover](faculty_gender_demographic_turnover.md) shapes observed representation.

## Caveats

- Name-based demographic inference can be harmful and inaccurate.
- Causal claims require designs that separate bias, selection, field composition, and opportunity structure.
