# Citation-Productivity Feedback

## Summary

Citation-productivity feedback is the mechanism by which early citation reception changes an author's chance of continuing to publish, creating reinforcement between paper attention and career output.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: author, paper, early citation pulse, publication continuation, promotion decision, tenure decision, or research-front participation.
- Typical representation: continued-publication probability conditioned on early citation reception or citedness category.
- Mechanism: early citation attention raises self-confidence, peer recognition, institutional support, and motivation, making later publication more likely.
- Empirical signature: authors whose initial work is cited are more likely to remain active than authors whose initial work receives little or no citation attention, after accounting for field and opportunity.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Connects [citation pull mechanism](citation_pull_mechanism.md) at paper level to author-level [cumulative advantage](cumulative_advantage.md).
- Provides a citation-based bridge to [recognition-career stabilization](recognition_career_stabilization.md), where recognition affects career continuity.
- Helps explain why citation distributions and author productivity distributions can reinforce each other rather than evolving independently.
- Gives a mechanism to test alongside [reputation effects](reputation_effects.md), [scientific career impact](scientific_career_impact.md), and [continuant collaborators](continuant_collaborators.md).

## Operationalization

- Identify early-career authors or first-paper cohorts in a disambiguated author-publication panel.
- Measure early citation reception using an initial citation window, field-normalized citation count, or early citedness indicator.
- Measure continuation through later publication, sustained activity windows, tenure-track retention, or exit from the publication record.
- Compare continuation rates within field, cohort, institution, advisor, team size, and publication venue strata.
- Separate the effect of paper-level citedness from author reputation, institutional resources, and coauthor prestige.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Price (1976) argues that citation as a cumulative-advantage process feeds back into an author's productivity.
- Price states that initial citation pulses can modulate author behavior, causing either continuation of publication or cessation.
- The paper links well-received initial papers to self-estimate, promotion, tenure, motivation, and circumstances that make another publication attempt more likely.
- Price also cites evidence that transient authors had much lower annual citedness than continuant authors, using that contrast as support for a strong relationship between citation and publication continuation.

## Caveats

- Early citations may proxy paper quality, topic demand, coauthor status, venue, or institutional support rather than directly causing continuation.
- Publication continuation can be censored by career breaks, sector moves, name changes, or incomplete database coverage.
- The mechanism should be tested with temporal ordering and matched comparisons before treating citation reception as causal.
