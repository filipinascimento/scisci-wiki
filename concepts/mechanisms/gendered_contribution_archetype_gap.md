# Gendered contribution-archetype gap

## Summary

Gendered contribution-archetype gap describes systematic differences in the role profiles assigned to scientists of different perceived genders, especially whether scientists appear in leadership, specialized, or supporting contribution profiles at the same career stage.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: scientist, career stage, perceived gender group, contribution archetype, or publication role history.
- Typical representation: stage-stratified archetype shares, gender-by-archetype tables, confidence intervals, or Sankey diagrams.
- Mechanism: if access to lead roles is unequal, contribution profiles can reproduce career inequality even when publication counts treat all coauthors symmetrically.
- Empirical signature: one gender is less represented in leader archetypes and more represented in specialized or supporting archetypes within the same career stage.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Adds a role-aware mechanism to [gender/race stratification in science](gender_race_stratification.md).
- Refines [task specialization](task_specialization.md) by showing that division of labor can be demographically patterned.
- Links [leader-specialized-supporting archetypes](../representations/leader_specialized_supporting_archetypes.md) to [responsible metrics](../measures/responsible_metrics.md).
- Helps explain why [coauthorship networks](../representations/coauthorship_networks.md) and publication counts can hide unequal access to leadership work.

## Operationalization

- Infer or observe gender with transparent uncertainty and coverage reporting.
- Assign contribution archetypes by career stage using observed or imputed contribution roles.
- Compare archetype shares within career stage rather than comparing raw counts across cohorts.
- Report confidence intervals, effect sizes, and coverage caveats for each gender-by-stage cell.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Robinson-Garcia et al. (2020) reports a consistent gender bias across contribution archetypes.
- The paper finds that men are more represented in the Leader archetype during early- and mid-career stages, while women are more concentrated in the Specialized archetype.
- Robinson-Garcia et al. report that 43% of men and 27% of women are Leaders in the early-career stage, and 77% of men and 65% of women are Leaders in the mid-career stage.
- The paper compares scientists within career stage to reduce generational confounding, noting that late-career gender shares are affected by cohort composition.

## Caveats

- Gender inference is not the same as self-identified gender and can introduce coverage and classification bias.
- Contribution profiles may reflect field, institution, team size, funding, family-care constraints, or author-order norms as well as gendered access.
- A specialized archetype should not be treated as lower intrinsic value; the mechanism concerns unequal recognition and career opportunity.
