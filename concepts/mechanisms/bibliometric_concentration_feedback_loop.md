# Bibliometric concentration feedback loop

## Summary

Bibliometric concentration feedback loop is the mechanism in which citation-based evaluation helps highly cited scientists obtain more resources, expand labs and collaborations, and accumulate still more publications and citations.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: author, lab, funding decision, hiring decision, citation indicator, collaboration network, or institution.
- Typical representation: reinforcing loop connecting citation indicators, grants, staff, collaborations, full-count output, and future citations.
- Mechanism: evaluation systems that reward past citation visibility can amplify already concentrated scientific attention and resources.
- Empirical signature: highly cited authors gain citation and publication share while collaboration networks and funding concentration grow.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Provides a mechanism-level extension of [citation elite concentration](../measures/citation_elite_concentration.md) and [citation elite productivity divergence](citation_elite_productivity_divergence.md).
- Connects [attention inequality](attention_inequality.md) to [funding incentives and exploration](funding_incentives_and_exploration.md), [scientific resource cumulative advantage](scientific_resource_cumulative_advantage.md), and [responsible metrics](../measures/responsible_metrics.md).
- Helps interpret why full-count publication and citation shares can increase for citation elites even without rising per-paper impact.
- Gives policy experiments a target: weakening or redesigning metric-mediated reinforcement in hiring, funding, and promotion.

## Operationalization

- Measure citation status before funding, hiring, or resource-allocation decisions.
- Track subsequent funding, lab size, collaborator counts, publication rates, and citation accumulation.
- Test whether bibliometric indicators mediate resource access and later citation concentration.
- Compare full-count and fractional-count outcomes to separate collaboration expansion from per-paper impact.
- Use quasi-experimental funding, hiring, or evaluation-rule changes where possible.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Nielsen and Andersen (2021) interprets rising citation inequality as aligned with self-reinforcing status hierarchies in science.
- They argue that highly cited authors have better odds of winning grants, which can let them expand research labs and collaborative networks, increasing full-count publication and citation rates.
- The same discussion notes that when funding and hiring decisions are heavily informed by bibliometric indices, both funding, publication, and citation concentration may further increase.
- Their empirical results show the descriptive components of this loop: the citation elite increases aggregate shares while annual collaborator counts and full-count output rise relative to lower-percentile authors.

## Caveats

- The Nielsen and Andersen evidence is interpretive for the feedback loop; causal identification requires independent funding, hiring, or policy shocks.
- Citation-based evaluation is only one possible driver of concentration; field fusion, big data, large-scale experiments, and computational infrastructure can also matter.
- Feedback loops can differ by field and institution, so global citation-elite trends should not be treated as direct proof of local evaluation behavior.
- A policy response should distinguish harmful concentration from legitimate coordination around large scientific infrastructure.
