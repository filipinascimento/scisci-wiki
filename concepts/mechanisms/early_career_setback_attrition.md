# Early-career setback attrition

## Summary

Early-career setback attrition is the mechanism by which a near-threshold early funding failure increases the probability that a scientist exits or disappears from the observed funding system.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: scientist, grant applicant, funding event, active-status year, or career trajectory.
- Typical representation: active-PI share, attrition rate, survival curve, disappearance probability, or retention gap.
- Mechanism: early resource loss and negative signals can reduce persistence, follow-on applications, institutional support, or ability to remain visible in the funding system.
- Empirical signature: near misses show lower future active-system participation than otherwise similar narrow wins.

## Operationalization

- Define active status, such as applying for or receiving NIH grants in the future.
- Compare active-status rates between near-miss and narrow-win groups over follow-up years.
- Estimate disappearance probability using the same threshold or matching design used for impact outcomes.
- Treat attrition as a substantive outcome, not only as missing data.
- Report outcome analyses both conditional on survival and with attrition explicitly modeled.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Wang, Jones, and Wang (2019) defines active PIs as those who apply for or receive NIH grants at some point in the future.
- The paper reports that in the year immediately after treatment, near misses had 11.2% fewer active PIs than narrow wins.
- The active-status gap persists beyond the first five years and is 11.8% in year seven.
- Wang et al.'s regression discontinuity analysis indicates that an early-career near miss led to a 12.6% chance of disappearing permanently from the NIH system over the next ten years.
- The authors use this attrition result to frame the fragility of junior scientific careers after early setbacks.

## Caveats

- Disappearing from the NIH system is not the same as leaving science entirely.
- Applicants may continue in industry, clinical work, teaching, non-NIH funding systems, or non-U.S. institutions.
- Attrition can be both a causal consequence and a selection filter for later observed performance.
- Active-status definitions should be reported and varied when possible.
