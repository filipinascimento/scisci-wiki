# Team-output full-credit inflation

## Summary

Team-output full-credit inflation is the validation problem that every member of a large collaboration can receive full publication-count credit for the same team output, inflating productivity tails.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: author, large team, collaboration, paper count, full-count credit, or productivity distribution.
- Typical representation: productivity-tail bump, full-count versus fractional-count comparison, or large-team sensitivity analysis.
- Validation target: determine whether extreme author productivity reflects individual output or team full-count attribution.
- Empirical signature: high-productivity tail mass decreases when large-team papers are fractionally counted or treated separately.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Complements [mega-collaboration degree inflation](mega_collaboration_degree_inflation.md), which focuses on coauthor degree rather than paper-count credit.

- Helps interpret team-science panels where large collaborations publish many papers under stable author lists.

## Operationalization

- Identify large-team or collaboration-authored papers.
- Compare full-count, fractional-count, and large-team-excluded productivity distributions.
- Test whether bumps or tail anomalies align with known collaboration output streams.
- Report field-specific authorship norms before interpreting extreme productivity.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Newman (2001) notes a bump in the SPIRES papers-per-author distribution and attributes it to members of large collaborations receiving credit for the collaboration's full output.
- The motif captures a full-count attribution artifact in productivity distributions.
- It is distinct from coauthor-degree inflation because the same large-team structure can inflate both degree and paper-count measures through different mechanisms.
- It is also distinct from version duplication, which double-counts records rather than full-crediting team output.

## Caveats

- Full-count credit may be appropriate for some recognition or participation questions.
- Fractional counting can understate genuine contribution in highly integrated teams.
- Large-team authorship conventions vary across fields and experiments.
