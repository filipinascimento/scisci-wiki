# Subfield citation inequality heterogeneity

## Summary

Subfield citation inequality heterogeneity is the validation finding that citation-concentration trends can differ substantially across disciplines and subfields even when the global trend rises.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: field, subfield, OECD major field, Web of Science category, author population, or time window.
- Typical representation: field-stratified top-percentile shares, Gini curves, or relative-change tables.
- Validation target: test whether an inequality conclusion is universal, field-specific, or driven by a subset of disciplines.
- Empirical signature: some fields show large increases in elite citation shares while others show weak increases or declines.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Adds field-level heterogeneity to [citation elite concentration](../measures/citation_elite_concentration.md).
- Prevents [attention inequality](../mechanisms/attention_inequality.md) from being interpreted as a single global law without field checks.
- Links citation inequality to field-specific publication, collaboration, funding, and citation customs.
- Complements [field-specific indicator suites](../methods/field_specific_indicator_suites.md) and [field-classification sensitivity](field_classification_sensitivity.md).

## Operationalization

- Define field and subfield classifications before measuring inequality.
- Estimate citation concentration separately by field and period.
- Report top-percentile share changes and Gini changes by field.
- Identify fields that drive the global trend and fields that deviate.
- Interpret field heterogeneity alongside differences in funding concentration, authorship norms, reference-list length, and cited half-life.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Nielsen and Andersen (2021) reports that citation inequality and concentration rise across agricultural sciences, medical and health sciences, and natural sciences after excluding physics and astronomy from detailed trends.
- The paper also reports strong subfield heterogeneity: citation concentration increases most in clinical medicine and health sciences, while computer and information sciences show a decrease.
- Nielsen and Andersen suggest that funding streams, citation practices, publication frequencies, author-group sizes, paper lengths, reference-list length, and cited half-life may help explain these differences.
- The result supports field-stratified inequality reporting rather than treating global concentration as field-invariant.

## Caveats

- Field taxonomies can hide within-field topic differences or split similar specialties.
- Field differences may reflect measurement conventions rather than true social inequality.
- Small subfields can produce noisy top-percentile estimates.
