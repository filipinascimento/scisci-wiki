# Bibliometric social-welfare boundary

## Summary

Predictive validity for papers, citations, and patents should be separated from broader social welfare or health outcomes.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: grant, publication, citation, patent, health outcome, survival outcome, disease burden, or welfare metric.
- Typical representation: outcome-boundary statement distinguishing bibliometric proxies from ultimate welfare targets.
- Mechanism, measurement, or validation target: validity boundary for grant peer-review outcome measures.
- Empirical signature: a peer-review score can predict bibliometric outputs without proving that allocation maximizes health or social welfare..

- Refines grant-review outcome validation by linking it to [grant peer review value added](grant_peer_review_value_added.md) and [direct indirect grant patent outcomes](../measures/direct_indirect_grant_patent_outcomes.md).

## Operationalization

- State whether the outcome is bibliometric, technological, clinical, health, distributional, or welfare oriented.
- Add patent, clinical, disease-burden, or health-outcome layers when policy conclusions require them.
- Avoid treating citation-prediction as equivalent to optimal public-value allocation.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Li and Agha (2015) says bibliometric outcomes may not perfectly capture NIH objectives and that health and survival outcomes would be ideal but difficult.
- The motif places grant-review validation inside a broader outcome hierarchy.

## Caveats

- Strong bibliometric prediction does not imply optimal allocation for health, equity, disease burden, or welfare.
- Downstream health outcomes are delayed, noisy, and hard to attribute to individual grants.
