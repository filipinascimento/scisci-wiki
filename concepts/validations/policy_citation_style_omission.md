# Policy citation-style omission

## Summary

Policy citation-style omission is the problem that policy documents may rely on scientific work without citing every relevant paper in a formal scholarly style.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: policy document, scholarly paper, mention event, evidence body, guideline, or report bibliography.
- Typical representation: missing formal citation, informal reference, bibliography gap, body-of-evidence use, or non-DOI reference.
- Validation target: whether absence of a tracked policy mention means absence of policy use.
- Empirical signature: policy documents can base arguments on broad bodies of work or reports while omitting individual scholarly citations.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Qualifies [policy document mentions](../measures/policy_document_mentions.md) as observed references, not complete policy use.
- Explains one mechanism behind low [policy mention coverage rate](../measures/policy_mention_coverage_rate.md).
- Complements [policy mention context audit](policy_mention_context_audit.md) by addressing false negatives, not only false positives.
- Connects policy metrics to [responsible metrics](../measures/responsible_metrics.md) by discouraging absence-as-no-impact interpretations.

## Operationalization

- Treat non-mentions as unobserved use rather than proof of no use.
- Audit policy documents for informal evidence references, report citations, literature summaries, and undifferentiated evidence bodies.
- Compare formal DOI references with broader textual discussion when full policy documents are available.
- Pair mention data with case studies, surveys, interviews, or policy-process evidence when evaluating societal impact.
- Report that DOI-only and bibliography-only workflows undercount informal policy use.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Haunschild and Bornmann (2017) lists policy citation practices as a possible reason for low policy-mention coverage.
- The paper states that authors of policy-related documents often are not researchers and that a scientific citation style should not generally be expected.
- Haunschild and Bornmann conclude that policy-related documents may not mention every important paper on which they are based.
- The same discussion notes that barriers and low interaction levels between researchers and policymakers can also reduce observed policy mentions.

## Caveats

- Citation-style omission is hard to quantify without full-document audits or policy-author evidence.
- Some policy documents do cite formally, so the problem varies by source type, country, document genre, and field.
- Informal evidence use is not necessarily stronger than formal citation; both require context.
