# Knowledge-integration proxy boundary

## Summary

Knowledge-integration proxy boundary warns that team membership, affiliations, collaboration traces, or topic breadth are not equivalent to evidence that research outputs integrate distinct knowledge traditions.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: paper, field, team, institution, benchmark panel, reference profile, or interdisciplinarity indicator.
- Typical representation: proxy comparison, cited-field profile, team-authorship share, specialization measure, or validation table.
- Validation target: distinguish epistemic integration from social, organizational, or topical proxies.
- Empirical signature: proxy indicators such as coauthorship or departmental diversity move differently from reference-based or content-based integration measures.

## Operationalization

- State whether the study measures knowledge content, reference diversity, team composition, affiliation spread, collaboration type, or topic specialization.
- Compare proxy metrics with reference-based or content-based measures where possible.
- Avoid treating team size, multi-affiliation authorship, or topic breadth as direct evidence of interdisciplinary integration.
- Report cases where social and epistemic indicators diverge.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Porter and Rafols (2009) defines interdisciplinarity as integration of information, data, techniques, tools, perspectives, concepts, or theories from multiple bodies of knowledge.
- The paper argues that indicators of interdisciplinarity should be based on research outputs because knowledge integration is an epistemic category.
- Porter and Rafols explicitly distinguish content-based integration from team membership, departmental affiliation, and collaboration proxies.
- Their empirical discussion also warns that increased coauthorship should not be equated with interdisciplinarity and that topic specialization is a different construct from disciplinary integration.

## Caveats

- Reference-based indicators are themselves proxies and can miss methods, data, tacit knowledge, or conceptual integration.
- Some social proxies may be valid for specific questions, such as collaboration policy or team diversity, if not mislabeled as knowledge integration.
- The boundary depends on the evaluation target: epistemic integration, organizational collaboration, and societal problem solving are related but distinct.
