# Mobility policy shock evaluation

## Summary

Mobility policy shock evaluation tests how travel bans, border restrictions, visa changes, geopolitical exits, or other policy shocks alter scientific mobility, collaboration, and impact.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: policy shock, country pair, mobile scientist, institution, visa group, collaboration edge, or publication outcome.
- Typical representation: event study, difference-in-differences, interrupted time series, route redirection analysis, or mobility-network robustness test.
- Mechanism or measurement target: consequences of restricting or redirecting scholar circulation.
- Empirical signature: treated country pairs, scholar groups, or mobility routes change relative to comparable unaffected units after a policy shock.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Applies [SciSci quasi-experimental policy evaluation](scisci_quasi_experimental_policy_evaluation.md) to international mobility and science-workforce policy.
- Uses [brain circulation networks](../representations/brain_circulation_networks.md) as the structural baseline for estimating route disruption and redirection.
- Evaluates whether changes in [mobile scholar citation premium](../measures/mobile_scholar_citation_premium.md), collaboration, retention, and field participation follow mobility restrictions.
- Connects science-of-science evidence to policy consequences for immigration, visa, funding, and international-collaboration systems.
- Tests whether substitute destinations have enough capacity to convert redirected flows into impact through [mobility restriction scientific capacity contingency](../mechanisms/mobility_restriction_scientific_capacity_contingency.md).

## Operationalization

- Identify a policy shock with affected and less-affected countries, institutions, fields, or scholar groups.
- Reconstruct pre- and post-shock mobility flows, collaboration edges, author affiliations, and citation outcomes.
- Compare treated routes to matched or synthetic controls using event-study, difference-in-differences, regression-discontinuity, or network-robustness designs.
- Track outcomes such as researcher flow, return mobility, collaboration continuity, publication output, citations, team composition, and route substitution.
- Model route substitution through [central-country mobility displacement](../mechanisms/central_country_mobility_displacement.md) when policy shocks affect highly central countries.
- Separate attraction shocks from return-policy shocks using [attraction-return policy asymmetry](../mechanisms/attraction_return_policy_asymmetry.md) and [job-conditioned return intention](../measures/job_conditioned_return_intention.md).

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Sugimoto et al. (2017) motivates mobility policy evaluation with contemporary travel bans, visa restrictions, Brexit, and other isolationist actions.
- The paper argues that internationally comparable mobility indicators are needed because mobility is growing, complicated, and increasingly threatened.
- Sugimoto et al. state that collaboration is often initiated and sustained through in-person interactions, so mobility restrictions may damage an international collaboration system.
- The paper explicitly proposes using bibliometric standards to test the effects of political changes in future decades, making mobility restrictions a science-policy evaluation target.

## Caveats

- Policy shocks can coincide with funding changes, institutional responses, economic shocks, or geopolitical shifts.
- Mobility restrictions can redirect flows to other countries rather than simply reducing total mobility.
- Redirected flows may not yield global gains when substitute destinations lack scientific capacity.
- Outcomes may take years to appear, and short publication windows can misclassify delayed effects.
