# High-touch funding scalability boundary

## Summary

High-touch funding scalability boundary records that intensive person-centered funding programs may depend on scarce expert attention, elite eligibility, and institutional legitimacy conditions that limit scale-up.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: funding program, review panel, scientist cohort, policy system, foundation, public agency, or evaluation process.
- Typical representation: scale-up caveat, expert-time constraint, elite-eligibility boundary, public-legitimacy constraint, or program-cost boundary.
- Validation target: prevent evidence from a small intensive program from being generalized directly to a national funding system.
- Empirical signature: the authors identify resource, eligibility, feedback-quality, or political constraints that would change the program if expanded.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Qualifies [HHMI-NIH incentive contrast](../methods/hhmi_nih_incentive_contrast.md) by separating evidence about treatment effects from evidence about scalable policy design.
- Links [funding autonomy and exploration](../mechanisms/funding_autonomy_exploration.md) to [funding-allocation design alternatives](../methods/funding_allocation_design_alternatives.md), where feasibility and administrative legitimacy matter.
- Complements [life-science generalizability boundary](life_science_generalizability_boundary.md) by adding a program-design external-validity boundary.
- Provides a warning label for [active science-policy experimentation](active_science_policy_experimentation.md): pilot effects are not enough when the treatment itself changes with scale.

## Operationalization

- Record program size, eligibility rules, funding intensity, feedback process, reviewer seniority, reviewer time, and administrative cost.
- Compare pilot and scale-up designs for changes in selection, review quality, monitoring, feedback, and political accountability.
- Test whether effects vary by applicant quality, institutional status, field, or resource need.
- Treat public-agency constraints, geographic spread, institutional diversity, and defensibility of decisions as design features rather than implementation noise.
- Separate "does this intensive program work for its participants" from "can this program be expanded without changing its mechanism."

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Azoulay, Graff Zivin, and Manso (2009) cautions that HHMI-style exploration incentives should not be read as a critique of NIH policy.
- The paper states that it is unclear how easily, and at what cost, the HHMI program could be scaled because eligibility is limited to scientists showing exceptional promise.
- Azoulay et al. also note that HHMI's detailed evaluation and feedback consumes substantial resources, especially the time of elite scientists serving on review panels, and that quality could degrade under drastic expansion.
- The conclusion adds that NIH faces political constraints, including broad institutional distribution and the defensibility of project-based decisions, that private foundations can ignore.

## Caveats

- A scalability boundary is not evidence that an intervention fails; it specifies what must be retested under expansion.
- Political accountability can be a legitimate public-funding constraint, not merely a bureaucratic friction.
- The relevant scale boundary may differ for fellowships, block grants, lotteries, prizes, and institutional funding.
