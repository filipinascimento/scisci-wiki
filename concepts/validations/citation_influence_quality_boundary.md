# Citation-influence quality boundary

## Summary

Citation-influence quality boundary is the interpretive guardrail that citation indicators measure scholarly diffusion or influence, not intrinsic quality, rigor, or long-term societal impact.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: paper, citation metric, evaluator, funding portfolio, program, or decision context.
- Typical representation: influence label, quality caveat, expert-review requirement, or metric-use boundary.
- Validation target: prevent citation scores from being used as direct proxies for quality or decision worthiness.
- Empirical signature: metric designers explicitly state that citation scores should supplement, not substitute for, expert judgment.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Sets responsible-use boundaries for [relative citation ratio](../measures/relative_citation_ratio.md).

- Generalizes to citation percentiles, field-normalized citation scores, and article-level influence indicators.

## Operationalization

- Label citation indicators as influence, attention, or diffusion measures unless directly validated against other outcomes.
- Pair citation scores with expert interpretation and context-specific evidence.
- State that metrics should not be primary sole criteria for funding, hiring, or quality assessment.
- Add outcome suites when the relevant goal is health, innovation, software, patents, or social benefit.
- Communicate uncertainty from citation dynamics, reputation, field norms, and early-stage discovery.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Hutchins et al. (2016) argues that RCR should primarily be considered a measure of influence rather than impact or intellectual rigor.
- The paper notes that citation success reflects social variables and reputation as well as intrinsic value.
- The authors state that broad adoption of any new metric should follow extensive testing and expert interpretation, and that RCR was not designed to assess individual productivity or science quality.
- They conclude that the metric should supplement, not substitute for, expert opinion in funding decisions.

## Caveats

- Influence can still be relevant evidence for evaluation when interpreted carefully.
- Quality, rigor, usefulness, and social impact require different evidence streams.
- Expert assessment also has bias and should be made accountable.
