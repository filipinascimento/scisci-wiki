# Publication-success screening bias

## Summary

Publication-success screening bias is the problem that published records underrepresent research effort because failures and unconventional experiments are less likely to become observable publications.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: attempted experiment, published paper, failed experiment, unconventional project, observed edge, or knowledge-network trace.
- Typical representation: publication-selection caveat, success-screening bias, missing-failure adjustment, or observed-effort boundary.
- Validation target: assess whether published traces are a sufficiently faithful proxy for underlying scientific choices.
- Empirical signature: risky or failed choices are underrepresented in the literature even though they shape the real search process.

## Operationalization

- Treat the published network as a selected sample of attempted research effort.
- Identify whether failures, negative results, or unconventional projects are observable.
- State assumptions about whether selection from attempted effort to publication is stable over time.
- Use surveys, lab records, preprints, registered reports, or failure repositories where available.
- Interpret inferred research strategies as published-trace strategies unless stronger effort data exist.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Rzhetsky et al. (2015) states that the empirical network of published chemical relationships is an imperfect sample of research effort.
- The authors note that effort is screened by experimental failure and by the greater challenge of publishing unconventional papers compared with incremental ones.
- They argue that publications overwhelmingly document successful experiments and almost certainly underrepresent risky but unsuccessful choices.
- A footnote states that their interpretation assumes the selection process is well behaved and near stationary so observed distributions track unobserved research effort.

## Caveats

- Selection bias can differ by field, journal, time, funder, and result type.
- Published traces can still be informative because scientists themselves use them to design new experiments.
- Correcting the bias requires data sources that may not exist for historical work.
