# Hot-streak observational causal boundary

## Summary

Hot-streak observational causal boundary is the validation warning that observed regularities around hot-streak onset identify associations, not proof that inducing the observed behavior will cause a hot streak.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: hot-streak onset, career trajectory, exploration/exploitation phase, collaboration change, omitted variable, or causal design.
- Typical representation: correlational-evidence caveat, omitted-variable audit, causal-design recommendation, or intervention boundary.
- Validation target: prevent descriptive career-event patterns from being interpreted as causal prescriptions.
- Empirical signature: the paper reports consistent associations but notes possible unobserved mediators such as funding, markets, networks, field culture, or feedback.

## Operationalization

- Label the evidence type: descriptive, predictive, quasi-experimental, experimental, or causal.
- Identify likely omitted variables around the career event.
- Test robustness with controls, individual fixed effects, randomization baselines, and subgroup splits.
- Avoid recommending forced behavioral changes unless supported by causal evidence.
- When policy implications are discussed, propose prospective tests or quasi-experimental designs.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Liu et al. (2021) states that the study presents correlational evidence and focuses on empirical regularities associated with hot-streak onset.
- The paper notes that causal research designs are needed to improve causal interpretations.
- Liu et al. discuss possible omitted or external factors, including market conditions, social network structure, disciplinary culture, and short-term feedback such as art critiques or peer reviews.
- The paper similarly notes that collaboration-pattern findings are observational and cannot rule out omitted variables.

## Caveats

- Strong cross-domain regularity increases interest but does not remove causal ambiguity.
- Randomized onset baselines and robustness grids are useful validation tools but not randomized interventions.
- Prediction tasks can be useful for analysis yet risky when used for evaluation, funding, or talent management.
- Causal designs may be hard because careers and creative strategies are not easily randomized.
