# Open science interventions

## Summary

Open science interventions are policy, infrastructure, and workflow changes designed to make research more transparent, reusable, credible, and less dependent on selectively reported results.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: journal, funder, laboratory, registry, manuscript, study, badge, policy, or adoption cohort.
- Typical representation: intervention portfolio, policy-adoption timeline, registry metadata, badge uptake, before/after reporting audit, or registered-report workflow.
- Mechanism or validation target: transparency requirements and incentive changes alter researcher behavior before, during, and after publication.
- Empirical signature: increased preregistration, data/code sharing, complete reporting, registered reports, disclosure compliance, and clearer separation of confirmatory and exploratory analyses.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Provides a policy and experiment layer for [replication and reproducibility](replication_reproducibility.md).
- Turns mechanisms such as [selective reporting and publication bias](../mechanisms/publication_bias_selective_reporting.md) and [analytic flexibility](analytic_flexibility_false_positives.md) into testable institutional reforms.
- Connects [peer review and gatekeeping](../mechanisms/peer_review_gatekeeping.md) to registered reports and in-principle acceptance.
- Gives [responsible metrics](../measures/responsible_metrics.md) a reform target: evaluate whether indicators and policies improve research practice, not just whether they are adopted.
- Provides implementation targets for [responsible indicators for assessing scientists](../measures/responsible_indicators_for_assessing_scientists.md), because promotion criteria can reward registration, sharing, and reproducible reporting.
- Separates researcher-assessment implementations into [complete transparent reporting rewards](../methods/complete_transparent_reporting_rewards.md) and [open research reward criteria](../methods/open_research_reward_criteria.md).

## Operationalization

- Track adoption of preregistration, registered reports, open-data badges, TOP guidelines, data/code availability requirements, reporting checklists, and registry mandates.
- Compare outcomes before and after adoption, across adopting and non-adopting journals or funders, or across manuscripts with and without badges or registrations.
- Measure compliance quality, not just the presence of a badge, statement, or registry link.
- Audit whether interventions change reporting completeness, positive-result share, replication success, data/code reuse, and correction behavior.
- For replication consortia, include [replication analysis reproduction audits](../methods/replication_analysis_reproduction_audit.md) and [replication feasibility attrition](replication_feasibility_attrition.md) in the implementation record.
- For discovery-pipeline reforms, distinguish published-success evidence from the [publication-conditioned SciSci blind spot](publication_conditioned_scisci_blind_spot.md).

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Nosek et al. (2018) frames preregistration as an intervention that protects the distinction between prediction and postdiction.
- The same full text describes journal and funder incentives, clinical-trial registration requirements, TOP Guidelines, [preregistration badges](preregistration_badge_incentives.md), monetary preregistration challenges, [registry services](../datasets/preregistration_registry_ecosystem.md), and registered reports as complementary adoption mechanisms.
- Nosek et al. describe registered reports as a workflow in which peer review evaluates the question and method before outcomes are known, then second-stage review checks execution and adherence rather than outcome appeal.
- Nosek et al. also identify the [novel-positive-clean result incentive](../mechanisms/novel_positive_clean_result_incentive.md) as the reward pattern that transparency reforms need to counteract.
- Verified full-text evidence from Simmons et al. (2011) supplies a lower-intensity intervention class: the [researcher-degrees disclosure checklist](../methods/researcher_degrees_disclosure_checklist.md), including stopping rules, variables, conditions, [exclusion sensitivity](exclusion_sensitivity_reporting.md), and covariate-free results.
- Simmons et al. also make two open-science boundary conditions explicit: [open data and materials insufficiency](open_data_materials_insufficiency.md) and the longer-term [public repository file-drawer extension](public_repository_file_drawer_extension.md).
- Verified full-text evidence from Fang et al. (2012) adds a correction-system intervention target: uniform standards for retraction notices and centralized misconduct data can improve post-publication accountability.
- Fang et al.'s proposed correction-system reforms are split out as a [research integrity intervention portfolio](../methods/research_integrity_intervention_portfolio.md), supported by [misconduct detection infrastructure](../mechanisms/misconduct_detection_infrastructure.md) and bounded by the [unretracted misconduct dark figure](unretracted_misconduct_dark_figure.md).
- Verified full-text evidence from Open Science Collaboration (2015) demonstrates a consortium-scale intervention: public protocols, original-author review, open materials and data, and project-wide audits made direct replications inspectable even when individual results were mixed.
- OSC also provides a policy-design motif: [innovation-verification resource balance](../mechanisms/innovation_verification_resource_balance.md), because incentives can be tuned to reward verification as well as novelty.
- Verified full-text evidence from Moher et al. (2018) turns open-science practices into assessment criteria: promotion and tenure committees could reward registration, registered reports, complete publication, data/code/material sharing, and open research milestones.
- Moher et al. also argue that such initiatives need evaluation components and audit-and-feedback loops to distinguish adoption from behavior change.

## Caveats

- Adoption is not compliance; a low-quality preregistration or empty data statement may not change evidence quality.
- Interventions can shift burdens unevenly across fields, resource levels, or study types.
- Transparency tools can make bias visible without eliminating it.
- Reform evaluation should distinguish symbolic uptake from changes in outcomes such as reporting completeness, replication, reuse, and correction speed.
