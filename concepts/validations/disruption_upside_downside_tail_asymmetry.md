# Disruption upside-downside tail asymmetry

## Summary

Disruptive work carries delayed upside but also greater downside risk, so disruption should be interpreted as a high-variance strategy rather than a universal quality premium.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: paper, disruption bin, citation tail, and time window.
- Typical representation: top-tail and bottom-tail citation probabilities by disruption level.
- Validation target: asymmetric risks and rewards of disruptive strategies.
- Empirical signature: disruptive papers gain long-run top-tail advantage while retaining elevated low-citation risk.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Connects [team-size disruption-impact tail divergence](team_size_disruption_impact_tail_divergence.md) to strategy-risk interpretation.
- Useful when policy discussions treat disruption as unconditionally desirable.
- Links disruption to [novelty impact dispersion](../measures/novelty_impact_dispersion.md).

## Operationalization

- Compare top-x and bottom-y citation probabilities for high- and low-disruption papers across time windows and team-size bins.
- Report both upside and downside tails rather than only mean impact.
- Separate published-paper risk from unpublished failure risk where traces exist.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Wu et al. (2019) shows top-1% citation advantage shifts toward disruptive work only over long windows, while bottom-10% citation risk is usually higher for disruptive work.
- The pattern supports a high-variance interpretation of disruption.

## Caveats

- Published-paper data still undercount failed attempts that never produce a paper.
- Citation tails can reflect field size, venue, and database coverage as well as strategy.
