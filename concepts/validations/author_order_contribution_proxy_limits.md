# Author-order contribution-proxy limits

## Summary

Author-order contribution-proxy limits are the validity problems that arise when byline position is used as a proxy for scientific contribution or leadership without checking field, career stage, team size, and role-specific contribution evidence.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: author-paper row, byline position, contribution role, career stage, team, field, or authorship norm.
- Typical representation: author-position share by archetype, first/middle/last role table, or role-position discordance rate.
- Validation target: determine when author order is a useful role proxy and when it mainly reflects seniority, convention, or team structure.
- Empirical signature: the same byline position maps to different contribution profiles across career stages or archetypes.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Qualifies [journal-specific author-order histories](../representations/journal_specific_author_order_histories.md) and other byline-based role measures.
- Protects [task specialization](../mechanisms/task_specialization.md) from treating author order as direct contribution evidence.
- Complements [alphabetical author-order chaperone null](alphabetical_author_order_chaperone_null.md), which handles fields where author order is not role-coded.
- Supports role-aware versions of [coauthorship networks](../representations/coauthorship_networks.md).

## Operationalization

- Compare observed or imputed contribution roles with first, middle, and last author positions.
- Stratify by career stage, team size, field, journal, and alphabetical-ordering norms.
- Treat first/last-author indicators as proxies only after validating them against contribution statements or role models.
- Report where author order reflects seniority more than contributorship.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Robinson-Garcia et al. (2020) relates career stage and author order to contribution roles.
- The paper states that first and last positions often reflect leadership in the studied biomedical context, but the mapping is imperfect and stage-specific.
- Robinson-Garcia et al. report that middle authors are generally less involved in writing and conception/design but are more associated with contributing resources.
- In the late-career stage, Leader and Supporting archetypes show similar author-position distributions, which the paper interprets as author position being more related to seniority than contributorship at that stage.

## Caveats

- Author-order norms differ sharply across fields, journals, and national traditions.
- Large consortia, alphabetical authorship, corresponding authorship, and equal-contribution notes can break simple first/middle/last role interpretations.
- Contribution statements and role prediction models have their own coverage and reporting limits.
