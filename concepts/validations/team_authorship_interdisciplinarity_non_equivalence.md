# Team-authorship interdisciplinarity non-equivalence

## Summary

Team-authorship interdisciplinarity non-equivalence is the caution that more coauthorship or larger teams should not be interpreted as more interdisciplinary knowledge integration without direct evidence from references, topics, methods, or fields.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: paper, team, field-year cell, coauthor count, cited-category profile, or interdisciplinarity score.
- Typical representation: team-size indicators plotted alongside reference-based interdisciplinarity indicators.
- Validation target: whether collaboration growth and cognitive integration are empirically distinct.
- Empirical signature: coauthor counts rise more strongly than integration scores, or field ordering differs between teamwork and citation diversity.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Keeps [team-authorship share](../measures/team_authorship_share.md) and [mean team-size growth](../measures/mean_team_size_growth.md) separate from [interdisciplinarity](../mechanisms/interdisciplinarity.md).
- Clarifies that [coauthorship networks](../representations/coauthorship_networks.md) measure social production traces, not necessarily cognitive distance.
- Supports policy evaluation when programs claim to foster interdisciplinary research through team formation.
- Provides a validation check for studies that use collaboration as a proxy for knowledge integration.

## Operationalization

- Compute team-size or coauthorship measures and reference/topic-based interdisciplinarity measures on the same paper sets.
- Compare trends, field rankings, and correlations rather than assuming one proxy stands for the other.
- Where possible, inspect author affiliations, departments, contribution roles, cited categories, and topic embeddings separately.
- Report when team-based and citation-based indicators disagree.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Porter and Rafols (2009) reports about 75% average growth in authors per paper across six benchmark fields from 1975 to 2005.
- The same paper reports large declines in single-authored papers, with all sampled fields except Mathematics below 10% single-authored papers by 2005.
- Porter and Rafols explicitly warn that increased team publishing should not be equated with interdisciplinarity.
- Their Integration-score results show a much more modest growth pattern than coauthor-count growth, supporting the non-equivalence of teamwork and cognitive integration.

## Caveats

- Some interdisciplinary research is team-based, and some single-author research is interdisciplinary.
- Coauthorship can reflect shared facilities, labor division, data access, or authorship norms rather than knowledge integration.
- Reference-based interdisciplinarity can miss interdisciplinary work visible in methods, instruments, or social organization.
- The validation should be field-specific because authorship norms differ strongly across domains.
