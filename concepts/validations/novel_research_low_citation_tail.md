# Novel research low-citation tail

## Summary

Novel research low-citation tail is the validation pattern that highly novel papers have greater upside but also a larger chance of falling into low-citation outcomes.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: paper, novelty class, citation percentile, subject category, or citation window.
- Typical representation: lower-tail citation probability, dispersion estimate, least-cited decile share, or high-risk/high-gain contrast.
- Validation target: whether novelty produces only higher mean impact or also greater downside risk.
- Empirical signature: highly novel papers show higher average or top-tail impact while also being more likely to appear among low-cited papers.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Gives a downside-tail validation for [novelty impact dispersion](../measures/novelty_impact_dispersion.md).
- Sharpens [novelty penalty](../mechanisms/novelty_penalty.md) by distinguishing risk from simple under-recognition.
- Helps funding and evaluation systems treat novelty as a portfolio-risk problem.
- Complements [novelty indirect big-hit stimulation](../mechanisms/novelty_indirect_big_hit_stimulation.md) by showing that indirect upside can coexist with direct downside.

## Operationalization

- Classify papers by novelty class within subject category.
- Estimate citation outcomes across both top and bottom percentiles, not just mean citations.
- Test whether highly novel papers are overrepresented in low-citation tails after field and paper-level controls.
- Compare lower-tail results across short and long citation windows.
- Report dispersion or variance estimates alongside top-percentile probabilities.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Wang, Veugelers, and Stephan (2017) reports that highly novel papers receive more long-run citations on average and have higher odds of becoming top-1% cited, but also show greater citation dispersion.
- The paper states that highly novel papers are more likely to be among the least 10% cited papers.
- Wang et al. frame this as a high-risk/high-gain pattern rather than a uniform novelty premium.
- The low-citation tail helps explain why individual-paper evaluation is difficult even when novelty has a positive average long-run association.
- The pattern supports portfolio-level evaluation of exploratory research rather than reliance on early paper-level signals.

## Caveats

- Low citation counts can reflect weak work, field norms, indexing issues, or delayed recognition.
- Bottom-tail shares depend on the field-year denominator and citation window.
- A risk profile is not itself evidence that every novel paper deserves positive evaluation.
- The pattern should be paired with direct, indirect, and qualitative evidence when evaluating specific papers.
