# Cause-specific correction priority

## Summary

Cause-specific correction priority is the validation rule that research-integrity interventions should weight correction causes by downstream scientific harm as well as by frequency.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: retracted paper, retraction cause, citation count, integrity intervention, journal policy, or correction portfolio.
- Typical representation: cause-by-impact table, priority matrix, highly cited retraction audit, or intervention triage rule.
- Validation target: prevent correction systems from treating all retraction causes as equally damaging to the knowledge base.
- Empirical signature: causes such as fraud or error dominate highly cited retractions more than plagiarism or duplicate publication.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Adds a harm-prioritization layer to [research integrity intervention portfolio](../methods/research_integrity_intervention_portfolio.md).
- Helps interpret [post-retraction citation persistence](../measures/post_retraction_citation_persistence.md) by asking which causes continue to propagate.
- Complements [retraction cause taxonomies](../methods/retraction_cause_taxonomies.md), which classify causes without necessarily ranking intervention urgency.
- Gives [responsible metrics](../measures/responsible_metrics.md) a guardrail before using raw retraction counts as a governance indicator.

## Operationalization

- Classify retractions by reconciled cause.
- Measure downstream harm proxies, such as citations before and after retraction, clinical or policy uptake, and centrality in derivative literature.
- Compare cause-specific distributions among highly cited retractions and ordinary retractions.
- Prioritize interventions that reduce causes producing greater knowledge contamination or downstream reliance.
- Report the difference between ethical seriousness, scientific-harm potential, and ease of detection.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Fang, Steen, and Casadevall (2012) reports that the 20 most highly cited retracted articles in their sample included no articles retracted for plagiarism or duplicate publication.
- The same discussion argues that plagiarism and duplicate publication should be discouraged but may have more limited scientific impact than fraud and error in this context.
- Fang et al. use this comparison to argue that reducing fraud and error should be a high priority for improving scientific integrity.
- This supports cause-specific intervention priority rather than treating each retraction cause as equivalent.

## Caveats

- Highly cited retractions are only one harm proxy; low-cited work can still cause clinical, policy, or public harm.
- Plagiarism and duplicate publication can damage trust, careers, and literature integrity even when their citation impact is lower.
- Priority rules should be revisited by field, period, and downstream-use setting.
