# Prediction-postdiction boundary

## Summary

The prediction-postdiction boundary separates tests specified before outcomes are known from explanations or analyses generated after outcomes have been observed.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: hypothesis, analysis plan, statistical test, result, manuscript claim, registry entry, or exploratory finding.
- Typical representation: confirmatory/exploratory label, preregistered plan, deviation table, or evidence-flow diagram.
- Mechanism or validation target: calibrated inference depends on knowing whether a claim was tested prospectively or rationalized retrospectively.
- Empirical signature: the same data both generate and evaluate a claim, but the published account presents the claim as if it was predicted in advance.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Gives [preregistration and registered reports](preregistration_registered_reports.md) a precise inferential target.
- Explains why [researcher degrees of freedom](researcher_degrees_of_freedom.md) and selective reporting inflate confidence in findings.
- Helps classify research outputs into hypothesis-generating and hypothesis-testing roles without devaluing either.
- Supports audit designs that distinguish planned analyses, transparent deviations, and post hoc exploration.
- Connects cognitive mechanisms such as [HARKing and hindsight bias](../mechanisms/harking_hindsight_bias.md) to statistical validation motifs such as [p-value diagnosticity under forking paths](p_value_diagnosticity_forking_paths.md).
- Connects to the [exploratory-confirmatory disclosure boundary](exploratory_confirmatory_disclosure_boundary.md) when the question is whether post hoc analyses were labeled, separately confirmed, or presented as prospective tests.
- Supports [exploration-to-prediction cross-validation](../methods/exploration_to_prediction_cross_validation.md), where a discovery subset generates claims and a held-out subset tests them prospectively.

## Operationalization

- Compare manuscript claims against preregistrations, protocols, time-stamped analysis plans, grant plans, or lab records.
- Label analyses as prediction, postdiction, or transparent deviation.
- Audit whether post hoc discoveries are reported as exploratory and followed by independent tests.
- Use registered reports or preregistered analysis plans to preserve the boundary before outcome observation.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Nosek et al. (2018) states that science alternates between generating hypotheses with existing observations and testing hypotheses with new observations.
- The same paper argues that mistaking postdiction for prediction reduces credibility because outcomes can shape explanations, model choices, and memory of what was expected.
- Nosek et al. connect the boundary to statistical inference: p-values and null-hypothesis significance tests are more interpretable when the tested analytic path was specified before observing the outcome.
- The same full text treats partial blinding, incremental preregistration, and deviation disclosure as ways to preserve some boundary information when fully prospective tests are not available.
- Nosek et al. also identify data splitting and cross-validation as ways to move discovery outputs into prediction tests, while warning that narrative interpretation can remain selective after planned analyses are reported.
- Verified full-text evidence from Simmons et al. (2011) shows the practical risk of a blurred boundary: undisclosed choices can make exploratory search appear confirmatory.

## Caveats

- Postdiction is scientifically useful for discovery when it is labeled as such.
- A preregistered prediction can still be weak if the design is underpowered or the analysis plan is vague.
- Existing data can support prediction only when the analysis choices are fixed before the relevant outcomes are inspected.
