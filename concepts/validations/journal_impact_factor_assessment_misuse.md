# Journal impact factor assessment misuse

## Summary

Journal impact factor assessment misuse is the validation failure that occurs when a journal-level citation average is used as a surrogate for the quality, impact, or promise of an article, scientist, or promotion case.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: journal metric, article, researcher, faculty dossier, hiring decision, promotion case, grant review, or institutional assessment.
- Typical representation: unit-of-analysis audit, citation-distribution skew check, policy review, or DORA-style assessment guideline.
- Validation target: whether a journal-level score is being substituted for paper-level or person-level evidence.
- Empirical signature: assessment criteria, CVs, letters, or committee practices use JIF thresholds, journal prestige, or journal rank as quality proxies.

## Operationalization

- Inspect hiring, promotion, tenure, grant-review, and award criteria for JIF thresholds or venue-prestige shortcuts.
- Check whether committee instructions prohibit or discourage journal-level metrics as article or researcher proxies.
- Compare journal-level citation distributions with article-level evidence to show why journal averages are weak surrogates.
- Replace JIF shortcuts with selected-work reading, article-level evidence, contribution statements, field context, and responsible indicator portfolios.
- Audit whether JIF language persists in letters, CVs, departmental templates, and informal decision narratives after formal policy changes.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Moher et al. (2018) identifies journal impact factor as a common but weak criterion in faculty assessment.
- The paper summarizes DORA as recommending against journal-based metrics such as JIF as surrogate measures of article or scientist quality in hiring, promotion, and funding decisions.
- Moher et al. state that current assessment often emphasizes easy-to-obtain measures, including JIF, while neglecting good practices such as reproducibility, transparency, and complete dissemination.
- The paper reports a skewness warning: roughly 10%-20% of papers can be responsible for 80%-90% of a journal's impact factor.
- Moher et al. therefore treat JIF de-emphasis as part of a broader responsible-assessment reform rather than as a purely technical citation-metric correction.

## Caveats

- Venue can carry limited contextual information about audience, review process, or field prestige, but it should not be used as a quality substitute.
- Removing JIF from formal criteria does not remove informal prestige effects unless letters, dossiers, and committee deliberations are audited.
- Article-level metrics can also be biased, so replacement indicators still need responsible-use checks.
- JIF misuse differs across disciplines because authorship, journal hierarchy, preprints, and conference systems vary.
