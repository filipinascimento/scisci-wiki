# Faculty prestige-merit conflation

## Summary

Faculty prestige-merit conflation is the validity problem that placement-derived institutional prestige mixes scholarly merit with social status, geography, resources, and other nonmeritocratic factors.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: institution, faculty placement, prestige rank, merit proxy, status signal, gender subgroup, or hiring network.
- Typical representation: caveat flag, merit/status decomposition, prestige-rank sensitivity, subgroup gap, or productivity-expected placement comparison.
- Validation target: prevent inferred prestige hierarchies from being treated as pure measures of quality or merit.
- Empirical signature: prestige predicts placement, but observed inequality is too steep or too subgroup-dependent to attribute confidently to merit alone.

## Operationalization

- State that inferred prestige is an operational status variable extracted from placements, not a direct quality measure.
- Compare hierarchy steepness with plausible productivity or merit differences when such proxies are available.
- Audit subgroup placement differences conditional on doctoral prestige.
- Test whether external merit proxies, candidate-level productivity, field demand, geography, mentoring, or resources explain prestige gradients.
- Avoid using prestige rank as a merit label for individual candidates, departments, or universities.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Clauset, Arbesman, and Larremore (2015) explicitly defines prestige in faculty hiring as an operational variable that can include scholastic merit and nonmeritocratic factors such as social status or geography.
- The paper argues that if prestige were equivalent to pure merit, nonmeritocratic factors would be irrelevant; more realistically, those factors weaken the correlation between prestige and merit.
- Clauset et al. note that the observed placement rates would imply top-10 institutions producing faculty who are two to six times more productive than faculty from the third 10 institutions under a pure meritocracy, which they describe as implausible.
- The paper also points to gendered placement differences in computer science and business as evidence that nonmeritocratic factors can shape placement quality.

## Caveats

- Prestige can contain genuine quality signals as well as status effects; the validation problem is conflation, not automatic invalidity.
- Merit proxies such as publications, citations, or awards have their own biases and coverage limits.
- Candidate preferences, geography, family constraints, subfield demand, and search timing can also shape placements.
