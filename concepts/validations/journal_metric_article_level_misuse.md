# Journal-metric article-level misuse

## Summary

Journal-metric article-level misuse is the error of using a journal-level average or prestige score as a proxy for the quality or influence of a specific paper.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: journal, article, author, citation distribution, evaluation decision, or metric application.
- Typical representation: warning that journal-level indicators should not be projected onto individual papers.
- Validation target: whether an evaluation use matches the unit measured by the metric.
- Empirical signature: journal citation distributions are highly skewed, so many individual papers differ sharply from the journal average.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Provides a responsible-use check for [journal impact factor](../measures/journal_impact_factor.md), [Eigenfactor Score](../measures/eigenfactor_score.md), and [Article Influence Score](../measures/article_influence_score.md).
- Links journal metrics to [responsible metrics](../measures/responsible_metrics.md), [indicator false precision](indicator_false_precision.md), and [citation metric gaming](citation_metric_gaming.md).
- Helps prevent journal-prestige substitution in hiring, funding, promotion, and paper evaluation.
- Links to [impact-factor publication pressure](../mechanisms/impact_factor_publication_pressure.md) when the journal-level average becomes a behavioral target.
- Connects to the more specific promotion-and-tenure failure mode in [journal impact factor assessment misuse](journal_impact_factor_assessment_misuse.md).
- Connects to [attention inequality](../mechanisms/attention_inequality.md), because journal-level citation distributions are heavy-tailed.

## Operationalization

- Identify the metric's unit of measurement before using it in evaluation.
- Compare article-level citation distribution within the journal to the journal-level score.
- Avoid assigning journal-average impact to individual articles, authors, or institutions.
- When article-level assessment is required, use article-level evidence, expert reading, or properly scoped article-level indicators.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from West et al. (2010) states that the only adequate approach to evaluating the quality of an individual paper is to read it carefully or consult others who have done so.
- In a note, the paper warns that a single paper's quality or influence is poorly estimated by the journal impact factor because citation distributions within journals are highly skewed.
- West et al. cite a Nature example in which 89% of the journal's impact factor came from 25% of its papers.
- The warning applies to journal-level Eigenfactor-family metrics as well: total and per-article journal indicators answer journal-level questions, not individual-paper quality questions.
- Verified full-text evidence from Moher et al. (2018) extends the warning to scientist assessment: DORA recommends not using journal-based metrics such as JIF as surrogate measures of article or scientist quality in hiring, promotion, and funding.
- Moher et al. also report the skewness problem in assessment terms, noting that a small minority of papers can account for most of a journal impact factor.

## Caveats

- Journal venue can carry contextual information, but it is not a substitute for paper-level assessment.
- Article-level citations can also be biased by field, age, team size, topic, and visibility.
- Reading every paper is infeasible for large-scale audits, so scoped quantitative proxies may still be necessary.
- The misuse check should be applied to any journal-level indicator, not only impact factor.
