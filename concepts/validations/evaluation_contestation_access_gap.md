# Evaluation-contestation access gap

## Summary

Evaluation-contestation access gap is the validation problem that evaluated researchers cannot challenge metric-based assessments when the relevant methods and evidence are scattered, obscure, or inaccessible.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: evaluation system, assessed researcher, metric, methodology document, institution, or contestation process.
- Typical representation: access audit, methods availability check, appeal workflow, documented indicator rationale, or evaluation-literature guide.
- Validation target: ensure that those affected by metrics can inspect and contest the methodological basis of assessment.
- Empirical signature: evaluation principles, data methods, and indicator limitations are available in forms that evaluated parties can find, understand, and use.

## Uses in Science of Science

- Extends [responsible metrics](../measures/responsible_metrics.md) from indicator design to contestability.
- Connects [transparent metric construction](../methods/transparent_metric_construction.md) with [evaluated-party data verification](evaluated_party_data_verification.md).

- Helps explain why codified evaluation principles matter even when specialists already know them.

## Operationalization

- Check whether indicator definitions, source data, formulas, and limitations are accessible to evaluated parties before decisions.
- Provide methodological summaries, appeal routes, and correction windows alongside evaluation outputs.
- Track whether contested evaluations identify data errors, invalid indicators, or unsupported interpretations.
- Audit access barriers such as paywalled scientometric literature, obscure venue placement, proprietary methods, and missing documentation.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Hicks, Wouters, Waltman, de Rijcke, and Rafols (2015) motivates the Leiden Manifesto as a codification of responsible metric principles.
- The article states that relevant principles were known to scientometricians but lacked a concise codified form for evaluation settings.
- Hicks et al. note that scientists seeking literature to contest evaluations may find the material scattered in obscure journals and inaccessible to them.
- The manifesto is presented as a way for researchers to hold evaluators accountable and for evaluators to hold indicators accountable.

## Caveats

- Access to methods does not guarantee that institutions will accept a contestation.
- Simplified guidance can omit important technical nuance if it is not linked to fuller documentation.
- Contestability requires time, administrative capacity, and power to pause or revise evaluation decisions.
