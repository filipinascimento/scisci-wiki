# Research assessment implementation experiments

## Summary

Research assessment implementation experiments evaluate whether new researcher-assessment criteria, dashboards, audits, or promotion policies actually improve scientific behavior and outcomes.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: assessment reform, institution, department, promotion committee, funder, researcher cohort, or policy rollout.
- Typical representation: cluster-randomized trial, stepped-wedge rollout, interrupted time series, audit-and-feedback intervention, baseline criteria audit, or before-after dashboard study.
- Validation target: whether assessment reform changes behavior, improves evidence quality, reduces harmful proxy use, or creates unintended consequences.
- Empirical signature: measured changes in registration, complete reporting, open data, open code, publication bias, reproducibility, societal uptake, JIF reliance, portfolio review quality, or gaming after implementation.

## Operationalization

- Establish a baseline by collecting current promotion, tenure, hiring, or grant-review criteria and making definitions explicit.
- Specify the assessment reform: JIF de-emphasis, RIAS dashboard, open-science rewards, portfolio review, audit-and-feedback reports, or revised CV format.
- Choose a design: cluster-randomized university or department rollout, stepped-wedge implementation, interrupted time series, matched institutional comparison, or mixed-methods audit.
- Measure both target outcomes and side effects, including behavior change, scientific quality, equity, workload, gaming, symbolic compliance, and career-stage burden.
- Publish criteria, implementation details, analytic code, and results so other institutions can reuse and test the design.
- Track whether broader criteria for outputs, mentoring, service, and team science are actually used in decisions.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Moher et al. (2018) states that most assessment proposals did not discuss how to evaluate the impact of adoption.
- The paper argues that new initiatives should include evaluation components, ideally using experimental methods, to determine whether they have intended effects.
- Moher et al. give a possible cluster-randomized trial in which universities are allocated to a data-sharing policy or standard practice, with later citation outcomes compared.
- The full text recommends documenting promotion-and-tenure criteria, using audit and feedback, and making institutional changes openly available so criteria changes can be tracked.
- Moher et al. identify stepped-wedge designs and interrupted time series as possible ways to assess departmental uptake of new scientist-assessment criteria.

## Caveats

- Randomizing institutions or departments can be politically and ethically difficult when careers are at stake.
- Outcome windows may be long, especially for reproducibility, societal impact, and risk-taking.
- Implementation effects can be confounded by simultaneous funder, journal, ranking, or national-policy changes.
- Better measured compliance does not necessarily mean better science; validation should include quality and unintended-consequence outcomes.
