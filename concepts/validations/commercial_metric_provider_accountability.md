# Commercial metric-provider accountability

## Summary

Commercial metric-provider accountability is the validation requirement that proprietary evaluation data and indicators expose enough coverage, protocol, and assumption detail for scrutiny before use.

## Canonical Form

- Unit of analysis: commercial metric platform, proprietary database, indicator provider, evaluation dashboard, coverage rule, or ranking product.
- Typical representation: provider documentation audit, coverage comparison, protocol transparency checklist, or black-box risk assessment.
- Validation target: determine whether an external provider's metrics are inspectable enough for consequential assessment.
- Empirical signature: data provenance, coverage, construction rules, corrections, and limitations are documented and open to challenge.

- Helps institutions decide whether a platform can support accountable hiring, funding, or ranking decisions.

## Operationalization

- Audit whether the provider discloses source coverage, document types, update cadence, matching rules, and normalization assumptions.
- Compare provider outputs against independent bibliographic sources for a sample of evaluated units.
- Record correction pathways and whether evaluated parties can inspect underlying records.
- Flag metrics whose formulas or coverage changes are undisclosed.
- Require documentation updates when provider products change.

## Evidence and Validations

- Verified full-text evidence from Hicks et al. (2015) rejects black-box evaluation machines and applies protocol standards to newer commercial metric entrants.
- The Leiden Manifesto treats transparency as a precondition for responsible use, especially when indicators enter formal evaluation.
- This motif isolates provider accountability from generic metric transparency because proprietary platforms can obscure database coverage and score construction simultaneously.
- It also provides a bridge between evaluation ethics and bibliographic data infrastructure.

## Caveats

- Full algorithmic disclosure may be limited by licensing or security concerns, but coverage and correction information can still be required.
- Open documentation does not ensure valid indicators; it only makes validation possible.
- Provider accountability should be revisited as databases expand, merge, or change source-selection policies.
